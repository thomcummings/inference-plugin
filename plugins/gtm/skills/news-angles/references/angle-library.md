# Angle library

Twelve ways a company's story gets told. Each entry says what the type is, when
it is news rather than an announcement, the proof it needs, who it usually
reaches, how it usually fails, and a worked example.

Every example uses the same invented company so the types can be compared:
**Pennant**, a made-up 40-person software company that sells scheduling
software to independent veterinary clinics. Pennant, its people, customers and
numbers are fictional.

Use the library two ways: to match inventory items to types (step 4), and to
teach. When you name an angle's type in the shortlist, add the one-line "when
it's news" test so the person learns to apply it.

## Vision piece

**What it is.** A clear claim about where the market is going and why,
usually under a named author: an op-ed, a keynote, a long post.

**When it's news.** When the claim is specific, a bit uncomfortable, and the
author has standing to make it. "AI will change vet clinics" is not a claim.

**Proof it needs.** A belief (from `point-of-view` if one exists), two or
three pieces of evidence the shift is real, and an author whose job or record
earns the right to say it.

**Usually reaches.** Trade press op-ed pages, industry newsletters,
conference programmes, the company's own channels.

**Usual failure.** A product pitch dressed as a prediction. If the piece only
works when the reader buys the product, it's an ad.

**Example.** Pennant's CEO argues that the independent clinic's biggest
threat isn't the corporate chains but its own appointment book: no-shows cost
more than chain competition, and the fix is operational, not marketing.

## Product launch

**What it is.** Something new that people can now use.

**When it's news.** When it does something nobody could do before, changes
the price or effort of something everyone does, or is the first of its kind
for a group of people. A feature at parity with competitors is an announcement.

**Proof it needs.** A demo or a working product, a before-and-after, and
ideally an early user who'll say what changed.

**Usually reaches.** Trade press, product newsletters and communities,
existing customers. Plan the launch itself with `release-gtm`.

**Usual failure.** Leading with the feature name instead of what changes for
the user.

**Example.** Pennant ships two-way text reminders that let pet owners swap
slots with each other. The angle isn't "Pennant launches SlotSwap", it's
"clinics are letting clients trade appointments like concert tickets".

## Partnership or integration

**What it is.** Two organisations doing something together.

**When it's news.** When the pairing is surprising, when one side is
well known, or when together they unlock something neither could do alone. An
integration listing on a marketplace is an announcement.

**Proof it needs.** A signed agreement both sides will name publicly, a
concrete joint outcome, a quote from each side.

**Usually reaches.** Both companies' trade press, both customer bases, the
partner's channels (often the bigger prize).

**Usual failure.** "Strategic partnership" with nothing anyone can use yet.

**Example.** Pennant and a (made-up) national pet insurer let owners book a
vet visit straight from an insurance claim. The story is the shorter path from
claim to care, not the partnership.

## Data story

**What it is.** Original numbers that tell people something about their world:
usage data, a survey, a benchmark, an index.

**When it's news.** When the finding is surprising or useful, the sample is
big enough to trust, and nobody else has the data. A number about your own
growth is a milestone, not a data story.

**Proof it needs.** The method (sample size, period, how it was collected),
anonymised and permitted data, one headline finding plus two or three
supporting ones. Have the analysis checked (`analyst`) before it goes out.

**Usually reaches.** Trade and business press, analysts, newsletters that
cite numbers, sales teams who reuse it for a year.

**Usual failure.** A survey designed to prove the product is needed. Reporters
spot it, and so do readers.

**Example.** Across bookings in Pennant's (invented) clinic base, Monday
morning no-shows run at twice the weekly average. The angle is "the Monday
problem", and it's useful to every clinic, customer or not.

## Milestone

**What it is.** A number or event the company reached: funding, customer
count, revenue, an expansion, an award.

**When it's news.** Rarely on its own. It becomes news when the number says
something about the market (the fastest-growing segment, a first in a
region), or when the funding comes with a clear story about what it's for.

**Proof it needs.** Numbers the company is genuinely willing to publish and
defend, and the "so what" for someone outside.

**Usually reaches.** Funding and startup press for funding; otherwise mostly
owned channels, customers, recruits.

**Usual failure.** Treating the milestone as the story. "Pennant hits 1,000
clinics" is an announcement; "one in eight independent clinics in the region
now books online, up from one in twenty" might be news.

**Example.** Pennant raises a (fictional) Series A. The angle is that
investors are backing independents against consolidation, with Pennant as the
example.

## Customer impact

**What it is.** A named customer's result, told as their story.

**When it's news.** When the result is big, specific and human, and the
customer is interesting in their own right (a first, an underdog, a turnaround).

**Proof it needs.** The customer's agreement to be named, a measured result
with a before-and-after, a person who'll talk.

**Usually reaches.** Trade press, local press (often overlooked), the
customer's own channels, sales.

**Usual failure.** A case study with the customer's name swapped in. The
product should be a supporting character.

**Example.** A two-vet rural clinic that nearly closed cut no-shows by half
and hired a third vet. The story is the clinic staying open.

## Founder story

**What it is.** Why the company exists, told through the person who started it.

**When it's news.** When the origin is unusual, ties directly to the problem,
or the founder has a perspective nobody else in the market has. Every founder
"saw a problem"; that's not a story.

**Proof it needs.** A true, specific origin moment, a founder willing to be
personal, and a link to why it matters now.

**Usually reaches.** Profiles, podcasts, startup and business press,
recruiting.

**Usual failure.** A biography. The story needs tension and a point.

**Example.** Pennant's founder ran the front desk at her parents' clinic for
six years and kept a paper log of every missed appointment. The log is the
story.

## Contrarian take

**What it is.** A public disagreement with something the market believes.

**When it's news.** When the belief is widely held, the disagreement is
backed by evidence, and the company can stand the pushback.

**Proof it needs.** The evidence, a named author, and a check that the take
fits the company's point of view rather than being a one-off provocation.

**Usually reaches.** Op-ed pages, newsletters, conference debates, social.

**Usual failure.** Contrarian in tone but not in content, or attacking a
straw man nobody holds.

**Example.** "Stop sending appointment reminders": Pennant's data suggests a
third reminder raises cancellations, so the industry habit backfires.

## Newsjacking

**What it is.** Adding something useful to a story that's already running.

**When it's news.** When the company has genuine expertise or data on the
running story and can respond in hours or days, not weeks. Needs the outside
scan (step 3); without it, don't guess at the news cycle.

**Proof it needs.** A spokesperson available now, a specific point or number
the existing coverage lacks, and a check that the story isn't one where
commercial commentary looks tasteless.

**Usually reaches.** Reporters already covering the story, newsletters,
social.

**Usual failure.** Slow, generic or self-serving commentary. If the comment
would fit any company in the category, it adds nothing.

**Example.** A national story breaks about vet staff shortages. Pennant offers
data on how many hours per week clinics lose to rebooking, and its CEO is
available that afternoon.

## Trend piece

**What it is.** The company as one example of a bigger shift, often alongside
others, sometimes competitors.

**When it's news.** When the shift is real, visible across several companies,
and not yet widely covered.

**Proof it needs.** Evidence the trend exists beyond this company, two or
three examples, and a reason the company is a credible voice on it.

**Usually reaches.** Feature writers, trade press, analysts.

**Usual failure.** A trend of one. If only this company is doing it, it's a
launch.

**Example.** Independent clinics are adopting tools built for restaurants and
salons (waitlists, deposits, reminders). Pennant is one of several examples.

## People

**What it is.** A notable hire, promotion, team or employee story.

**When it's news.** When the person is known in the field, the move signals a
strategic shift, or the team does something unusual.

**Proof it needs.** The person's agreement, the reason the move matters, a
quote.

**Usually reaches.** Trade press people columns, recruiting channels, owned.

**Usual failure.** A routine hire written up as a coup.

**Example.** Pennant hires a former head of a veterinary nurses' association
as its clinical lead. The angle is software companies hiring clinicians to
design for them.

## Behind the scenes

**What it is.** How the company built, decided or learned something.

**When it's news.** When the how is useful to others in the field, or
genuinely surprising, and the company is candid about what went wrong.

**Proof it needs.** A real decision, mistake or method, and permission to
share it.

**Usually reaches.** Practitioner newsletters, podcasts, communities, the
company's own engineering or design blog, recruiting.

**Usual failure.** Polished to the point of saying nothing.

**Example.** Why Pennant removed its most-requested feature after six weeks,
with the usage numbers that made the call.

## Combining types

Small items often combine into one stronger angle. Common pairings:

- **Data story + trend piece.** Own data proving a shift others are seeing.
- **Customer impact + milestone.** The milestone told through the customers
  who make it up.
- **Founder story + vision piece.** The founder's past explains the belief.
- **Launch + data story.** The data that showed the need, then the product.
- **Newsjacking + contrarian take.** A running story where the company
  disagrees with the consensus, with evidence.

When combining, the hook comes from the stronger type and the other supplies
proof.
