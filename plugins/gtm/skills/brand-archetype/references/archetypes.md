# The 12 Brand Archetypes — Reference

The 12 archetypes, organised by the four core human motivations they serve. Each entry has:

- **Motto** — the worldview compressed to one phrase.
- **Goal** — what the archetype is trying to achieve.
- **Strengths / voice** — the signal qualities of the archetype.
- **Brand examples** — companies that play this archetype well, with one-liner reasons.
- **Tells** — signals in a company's strategy or behaviour that suggest this archetype fits.
- **Failure modes** — how the archetype goes wrong (the more useful half of the entry, usually).
- **Often confused with** — adjacent archetypes that are easy to mistake for this one, and how to tell them apart.

## Seek Paradise

The motivation: a better, freer, truer world. These archetypes promise transcendence — escape from limits, ignorance, or compromise.

### 1. The Innocent

Also known as: Child, Optimist, Muse, Traditionalist, Saint, Angel.

- **Motto:** "Life is good."
- **Goal:** A happy, simple, pure life. Spread optimism. Do the right thing.
- **Strengths:** Sweetness, charm, sense of wonder, warm-heartedness.
- **Voice:** Simple, concise, cheerful, optimistic. No cynicism, no irony.
- **Visual world:** Light, soft, clean, natural, often white or pastel. Open skies, smiling faces, nature, children.
- **Brand examples:**
  - **Dove** — "real beauty" without complication or aspiration.
  - **Coca-Cola** — happiness as a universal birthright.
  - **Method / The Honest Company** — the wholesome alternative to industrial cynicism.
- **Tells in strategy:** Talks about goodness, purity, simplicity, "doing the right thing", "back to basics." Often consumer-facing. Hates complexity.
- **Failure modes:**
  - **Saccharine** — so positive it reads as fake or condescending.
  - **Naïve** — refusing to acknowledge real problems the customer has.
  - **Generic** — every Innocent looks the same (sunshine, fields, white packaging).
- **Often confused with:** Citizen (Innocent is purer/more optimistic, Citizen is more grounded and humble) and Caregiver (Innocent is *about* purity, Caregiver is *about* service to others).

### 2. The Sage

Also known as: Philosopher, Teacher, Guru, Expert, Intellectual.

- **Motto:** "To know is to grow."
- **Goal:** Understand the truth. Teach others what you know.
- **Strengths:** Objectivity, diligence, detail-orientation, deep expertise.
- **Voice:** Dignified, wise, curious, intelligent. Comfortable saying "we don't know yet" when honest.
- **Visual world:** Restrained, considered, often serif typography, lots of negative space, muted palettes. Libraries, dashboards, diagrams.
- **Brand examples:**
  - **The Economist** — "I'm not the smartest, but I read The Economist."
  - **Mayo Clinic / NYT / Harvard** — institutional authority earned through rigour.
  - **Bloomberg / Stripe Press / Wirecutter** — depth and methodology as the product.
- **Tells in strategy:** Built on research, data, methodology, "we did the homework", proprietary frameworks, education-led marketing, "the truth about X" tone.
- **Failure modes:**
  - **Boring** — so measured it has no pulse.
  - **Pedantic** — over-explains, over-qualifies, talks down.
  - **Cold** — forgets that knowledge serves humans.
- **Often confused with:** Magician (Sage explains the truth, Magician *causes* transformation) and Ruler (Sage's authority comes from knowledge, Ruler's from power/order).

### 3. The Explorer

Also known as: Pioneer, Adventurer, Seeker, Iconoclast, Individualist.

- **Motto:** "Live adventurously."
- **Goal:** Find out who you are by exploring the world. Blaze a new trail.
- **Strengths:** Self-motivation, ambition, authenticity, curiosity.
- **Voice:** Open-minded, resourceful, cultured, freeing. Often second-person ("your journey", "wherever you go").
- **Visual world:** Wide landscapes, road, horizon, weathered textures, photography over illustration, outdoor palettes.
- **Brand examples:**
  - **Patagonia** — purpose-led freedom in the wilderness.
  - **Jeep / Land Rover** — vehicles as permission to go anywhere.
  - **Airbnb (early era) / Away** — travel as self-discovery.
- **Tells in strategy:** Independence, autonomy, "the path less travelled", customers framed as seekers/curious/restless, anti-mass-market.
- **Failure modes:**
  - **Generic adventure aesthetic** — every Explorer brand has a mountain and a campfire.
  - **Performative authenticity** — explorer cosplay (handwritten fonts, faux-rugged).
  - **Aloof** — too detached, no real product clarity.
- **Often confused with:** Rebel (Explorer leaves the mainstream, Rebel *attacks* it) and Hero (Explorer is about self-discovery, Hero is about overcoming external challenge).

## Leave a Mark

The motivation: change the world or master it. These archetypes are wired for impact — they want to disrupt, transform, or conquer something.

### 4. The Rebel (Outlaw)

Also known as: Maverick, Game-changer, Outlaw, Challenger, Revolutionary, Misfit.

- **Motto:** "Rules are made to be broken."
- **Goal:** Rise up. Challenge the status quo. Disrupt what's broken.
- **Strengths:** Confidence, risk-taking, charisma, street smarts.
- **Voice:** Bold, candid, raw, honest. Will name the enemy explicitly.
- **Visual world:** High contrast, black-heavy, oversized type, irreverent layouts, graffiti energy, "punk" sensibility.
- **Brand examples:**
  - **Harley-Davidson** — freedom-as-defiance, identity in opposition.
  - **Liquid Death** — water that mocks the wellness industry.
  - **Oatly** — every package picks a fight with the dairy lobby.
  - **Apple's "Think Different" era** — the canonical mainstream Rebel.
- **Tells in strategy:** Explicit antagonist ("Big Pharma", "the legacy way", "corporate bullshit"), customers framed as misfits, "we exist because [incumbent] is wrong", contrarian product or pricing decisions.
- **Failure modes:**
  - **Cosplay** — performing rebellion without actually disrupting anything.
  - **Try-hard** — every piece of copy strains to be edgy.
  - **One-note** — the brand can only fight; doesn't know how to build.
  - **Punching down** — rebellion read as cruelty rather than liberation.
- **Often confused with:** Explorer (Rebel attacks the centre, Explorer leaves it behind) and Magician (Rebel breaks the system, Magician transforms it).

### 5. The Magician

Also known as: Visionary, Transformer, Wizard, Engineer, Catalyst.

- **Motto:** "Make dreams come true."
- **Goal:** Transform situations. Make visions reality. Help others turn ideas into action.
- **Strengths:** Empowering, charismatic, fascinating, spirited.
- **Voice:** Expansive, moving, articulate, story-rich. Talks about transformation, possibility, "what if."
- **Visual world:** Bold gradients, light, motion, before/after, sense of unveiling. Cinematic where possible.
- **Brand examples:**
  - **Disney** — the canonical "dreams come true" brand.
  - **Apple (post-iPhone)** — technology as everyday magic.
  - **TED** — "ideas worth spreading" as transformational ritual.
  - **Tesla / SpaceX** — Magician at scale (the future, made present).
- **Tells in strategy:** Talks about transformation, before/after, possibility, the future, "what if we could…" Often founders with a mythic backstory. Heavy demo-driven marketing.
- **Failure modes:**
  - **Vapourware vibe** — promises so big the product can't catch up.
  - **Cult-leader energy** — when the Magician story serves the founder more than the customer.
  - **Mysticism without substance** — "magical" copy with no actual transformation underneath.
- **Often confused with:** Hero (Magician transforms the world, Hero conquers it) and Creator (Magician makes change happen, Creator makes things exist).

### 6. The Hero

Also known as: Protector, Defender, Dragon Slayer, Champion, Warrior.

- **Motto:** "Where there's a will, there's a way."
- **Goal:** Save the day. Protect others. Achieve great things through courage and discipline.
- **Strengths:** Bravery, self-discipline, directness.
- **Voice:** Motivational, confident, empowering. Imperative verbs ("Do it", "Just go").
- **Visual world:** Strong silhouettes, action photography, high contrast, sweat, dust, finish lines.
- **Brand examples:**
  - **Nike** — "Just do it." The Hero archetype's textbook expression.
  - **Under Armour / Gatorade** — performance as virtue.
  - **U.S. Army / Marines** — Hero as institution.
  - **Whoop / Strava** — Hero translated for the data-driven generation.
- **Tells in strategy:** Performance, achievement, "for the ambitious", overcoming obstacles, framing the customer as someone with a mission to accomplish.
- **Failure modes:**
  - **Self-important** — earnestness tips into pomposity.
  - **Toxic-masculine** — Hero gets read as macho rather than courageous.
  - **Exhausting** — every interaction demands effort from the customer.
- **Often confused with:** Magician (Hero acts, Magician transforms), Ruler (Hero strives upward, Ruler already commands), and Rebel (Hero fights for the establishment's best version, Rebel fights against it).

## Connect with Others

The motivation: belonging, intimacy, joy. These archetypes are about people-with-people — closeness, love, laughter, community.

### 7. The Lover

Also known as: Romantic, Hedonist, Sensualist, Pleasure-Seeker.

- **Motto:** "Follow your bliss."
- **Goal:** Achieve intimacy, beauty, pleasure. Build deep relationships.
- **Strengths:** Passion, charisma with people, commitment, spellbinding presence.
- **Voice:** Affectionate, sensory, beautiful, emotional. Adjectives carry weight.
- **Visual world:** Warm palettes, soft light, intimate framing, tactile materials, beautiful objects, romantic typography.
- **Brand examples:**
  - **Chanel / Tiffany & Co. / Hermès** — luxury as intimacy with beauty.
  - **Aesop** — sensory product copy elevated to literature.
  - **Le Creuset** — the home as an act of love.
  - **Glossier** — beauty as friendship and pleasure.
- **Tells in strategy:** Sensory product (cosmetics, fashion, food, fragrance, hospitality, jewellery). Emotional purchase logic. "Treat yourself." Strong aesthetic standards. Romance, beauty, or care central to the offer.
- **Failure modes:**
  - **Kitsch** — over-sweetened, performatively romantic.
  - **Cloying** — too much; the customer wants air.
  - **Inappropriate context** — Lover energy in a B2B SaaS pitch reads as weird.
- **Often confused with:** Caregiver (Lover is about *desire and beauty*, Caregiver is about *need and care*) and Innocent (Lover is sensual, Innocent is pure).

### 8. The Jester

Also known as: Entertainer, Jokester, Goofball, Provocateur, Performer.

- **Motto:** "Have fun."
- **Goal:** Live in the moment. Lighten the world. Entertain through humour and play.
- **Strengths:** Life of the party, makes fun happen, energetic, entertaining.
- **Voice:** Funny, witty, enthusiastic, self-deprecating. Not afraid to look silly.
- **Visual world:** Bright, irreverent, mascot-friendly, willing to be weird, type and colour as comedy.
- **Brand examples:**
  - **Ben & Jerry's** — ice cream as joy and mischief.
  - **Skittles / Old Spice** — absurdism as identity.
  - **Cards Against Humanity / Liquid Death (also Rebel)** — surreal humour as brand strategy.
  - **Mailchimp (early era) / Slack (early era)** — playful B2B as differentiator.
- **Tells in strategy:** Self-aware humour, willingness to be silly, "we don't take ourselves too seriously" (when actually true), category that's normally serious or sterile and ripe for levity.
- **Failure modes:**
  - **Cringe** — humour that doesn't land becomes painful.
  - **Insubstantial** — all jokes, no real substance; can't sell a serious product.
  - **Inconsistent** — sometimes funny, sometimes corporate; the audience loses the thread.
- **Often confused with:** Rebel (Jester laughs at the system, Rebel attacks it) and Citizen (Jester is everyone's funny friend, Citizen is everyone's reliable friend).

### 9. The Citizen (Everyman)

Also known as: Everyman, Boy/Girl Next Door, Egalitarian, Regular Joe, Best Friend.

- **Motto:** "All for one and one for all."
- **Goal:** Belonging. Be the brand for everyone. Dignity and unpretentiousness.
- **Strengths:** Values-driven, accessible, practical, warm.
- **Voice:** Friendly, humble, empathetic, inclusive. No jargon, no airs.
- **Visual world:** Approachable, grounded, photography of real people, unpretentious type, primary colours often.
- **Brand examples:**
  - **Target / IKEA / Trader Joe's** — accessible quality without snobbery.
  - **Costco / Aldi** — the everyman's value proposition made into a religion.
  - **Habitat for Humanity / Gap** — community and dignity at the centre.
- **Tells in strategy:** "For everyone", accessible price points, anti-elitism, community-led, "we're a team here", inclusive language.
- **Failure modes:**
  - **Vanilla** — so middle-of-the-road it disappears.
  - **Fake-folksy** — performative humility from a corporation that's clearly anything but.
  - **Aimless** — "for everyone" becomes "for no one in particular".
- **Often confused with:** Caregiver (Citizen is *one of us*, Caregiver *helps us*) and Innocent (Citizen is humble and grounded, Innocent is naive and pure).

## Provide Structure

The motivation: order, care, control, craft. These archetypes build the world rather than transcend or transform it.

### 10. The Caregiver

Also known as: Nurturer, Mother, Altruist, Helper.

- **Motto:** "Love your neighbour as yourself."
- **Goal:** Help and heal others. Provide for needs. Build structures that sustain life.
- **Strengths:** Compassion, generosity, selflessness, empathy.
- **Voice:** Considerate, thoughtful, kind, encouraging. Genuine warmth, not corporate-warmth.
- **Visual world:** Soft, warm, human-centred photography, gentle palettes, often blues/greens, holding hands, hands-on care.
- **Brand examples:**
  - **Johnson & Johnson** — "a family company."
  - **TOMS / Warby Parker (give-one) / Allbirds** — care as model.
  - **Médecins Sans Frontières / Red Cross** — Caregiver at institutional scale.
  - **Calm / Headspace** — Caregiver for the mental health era.
- **Tells in strategy:** Service, care, health, education, well-being. Customers framed as people in need. Mission-led, often non-profit-adjacent. "We're here for you" tone.
- **Failure modes:**
  - **Self-righteous** — care becomes moral superiority.
  - **Paternalistic** — talks down to the customer.
  - **Burnt out** — Caregiver brands sometimes martyr themselves into invisibility.
- **Often confused with:** Citizen (Caregiver *serves*, Citizen *belongs to*) and Lover (Caregiver responds to *need*, Lover responds to *beauty*).

### 11. The Ruler

Also known as: Boss, Leader, Power Broker, King or Queen, Chief.

- **Motto:** "Control is power."
- **Goal:** Create order, prosperity, stability. Lead. Set the standard.
- **Strengths:** Productive, responsible, logical, confident.
- **Voice:** Refined, articulate, authoritative. Comfortable making declarations.
- **Visual world:** Restrained luxury, serif type, gold, navy, deep greens, symmetry, heritage cues, considered photography.
- **Brand examples:**
  - **Rolex / Rolls-Royce / Louis Vuitton** — luxury as authority and heritage.
  - **American Express / Goldman Sachs** — financial Ruler — trust, prestige, the seal of approval.
  - **The Economist (also Sage) / FT** — institutional voice of authority.
  - **Mercedes-Benz** — engineering as command.
- **Tells in strategy:** Premium pricing, heritage, "the standard", "trusted by [important institutions]", focus on quality and reliability, audience defined as serious/successful/discerning.
- **Failure modes:**
  - **Aloof** — so polished it becomes inhuman.
  - **Snobbish** — quality positioning tips into exclusion.
  - **Stuffy** — heritage cues drift into mausoleum.
  - **Performance-of-luxury** — Ruler signifiers without Ruler substance.
- **Often confused with:** Hero (Ruler already commands, Hero is still striving) and Sage (Ruler's authority is positional, Sage's is epistemic).

### 12. The Creator

Also known as: Innovator, Inventor, Designer, Artist, Craftsman.

- **Motto:** "Imagine the possibilities."
- **Goal:** Realise a vision. Make things of enduring beauty and value. Refuse mediocrity.
- **Strengths:** Creative, imaginative, visionary, quirky, individual.
- **Voice:** Descriptive, visual, symbolic. Loves specifics — materials, processes, design decisions.
- **Visual world:** Considered, often editorial, lots of craft cues, willingness to be idiosyncratic, design-led layouts.
- **Brand examples:**
  - **Lego** — creativity as the product itself.
  - **Adobe** — tools for makers.
  - **Apple (across eras)** — Creator energy in everything from packaging to typography.
  - **A24 / Studio Ghibli / Pixar** — creator brands as auteur brands.
  - **MUJI / Aesop / Muji-adjacent design brands** — craft and restraint as identity.
- **Tells in strategy:** Design-led, craft-led, "we built this differently", obsession with materials/process/quality, founder is a maker/designer/engineer, customers framed as fellow makers.
- **Failure modes:**
  - **Precious** — taste signalling that excludes more than it invites.
  - **Process-obsessed** — talks endlessly about craft, never about the customer.
  - **Inscrutable** — design that doesn't communicate.
- **Often confused with:** Magician (Creator *makes things*, Magician *makes change*) and Ruler (Creator's authority comes from craft, Ruler's from command).

## How to use this reference when scoring

When scoring all 12 archetypes against the strategic context:

1. **Read the "Tells in strategy" line for each.** That's the fastest fit check.
2. **Then check the "Failure modes" line.** If a brand is already failing in that direction, it's a flag that they're *being* that archetype, even if they don't realise.
3. **Don't over-weight brand examples.** The user's company is not those companies. Use the examples to clarify the archetype's flavour, not as a target.
4. **Pay attention to the "Often confused with" lines.** Most archetype mistakes happen because two adjacent archetypes were not distinguished cleanly.
5. **The "Voice" lines are the fastest reality check.** If the company's actual copy or behaviour can't plausibly speak in that voice, the archetype probably doesn't fit — even if the strategy looks aligned on paper.
