# Mode 3: Persona & Competitive Messaging

## 1. Why Persona-Level Messaging Matters

Company messaging and product messaging speak to "the customer" as a monolith. In reality, B2B buying involves multiple stakeholders with different concerns:

- The **economic buyer** (VP/C-level) cares about business outcomes, ROI, risk
- The **technical evaluator** (engineer, researcher) cares about capabilities, integration, reliability
- The **end user** cares about ease of use, daily workflow impact, learning curve
- The **champion** (internal advocate) needs ammunition to sell internally

The same product, described with the same value proposition, needs different emphasis, language, and proof for each persona. A data quality message lands differently with a VP of Engineering ("reduce costly model retraining cycles") than with a researcher ("ensure your results are reproducible").

### When Persona Messaging Is Worth the Investment

- The buying process involves 3+ stakeholders
- Different personas evaluate different criteria
- Sales teams report needing "different versions" of the pitch
- Website conversion varies dramatically by role
- The company sells to multiple distinct segments (e.g., researchers AND AI developers)

## 2. Building Persona Message Maps

A persona message map takes each persona and adapts the core messaging to their specific concerns, language, and decision criteria.

### Step 1: Define the Personas

For each persona, capture:

```
PERSONA: [Role / Title]
├── Who they are (role, seniority, function)
├── What they care about (top 3 priorities in their job)
├── What they fear (top 3 risks they're trying to avoid)
├── How they evaluate solutions (criteria they use to compare options)
├── What language they use (terms, jargon, level of technicality)
├── Where they get information (channels, publications, communities)
└── Their role in the buying process (economic buyer, evaluator, user, champion)
```

**Important:** Don't invent personas — derive them from sales conversations, customer interviews, and CRM data. If that data doesn't exist, build hypothesis personas and flag them for validation.

### Step 2: Map Core Messages to Each Persona

Take each message from the company and product messaging houses and ask: **"How does this message change for this persona?"**

Not every message needs a persona variant. Some messages are universal. The ones that need adapting are typically:

- **Value propositions** (different outcomes matter to different roles)
- **Benefit messages** (different benefits resonate with different roles)
- **Proof points** (different evidence is persuasive to different roles)

### Step 3: Write the Persona-Specific Messages

For each persona × core message combination:

```
PERSONA: [VP of Engineering]
CORE MESSAGE: "Invoices approved on time, every time" (fictional, Ledgerly)

PERSONA VARIANT:
  Headline: "Stop building approval workflows your finance team keeps breaking"
  Supporting: "Every custom approval script is another thing engineering has to
  maintain. Ledgerly's routing rules and API mean finance owns the workflow and
  your team owns nothing."
  Proof: "[Customer X] retired three internal approval tools after
  switching to Ledgerly."
```

### Persona Message Map Template

```
PERSONA MESSAGE MAP: [Persona Name / Role]

Role in buying process: [Economic buyer / Evaluator / User / Champion]

Top concerns:
1. [Concern]
2. [Concern]  
3. [Concern]

Adapted value proposition:
[Value prop rewritten for this persona's concerns and language]

Key benefits (prioritised for this persona):
1. [Most important benefit for this persona] — [why they care]
2. [Second most important benefit] — [why they care]
3. [Third most important benefit] — [why they care]

Key proof points (most persuasive for this persona):
1. [Evidence type and detail]
2. [Evidence type and detail]

Objections this persona raises:
1. [Common objection] → [Response framework]
2. [Common objection] → [Response framework]

Language notes:
- Uses [technical/business/academic] language
- Responds to [metrics/stories/technical detail]
- Avoid [specific terms or framings that don't land]
```

## 3. Mapping Messages to the Buyer Journey

Different messages land differently depending on where the buyer is in their journey. This mapping ensures the messaging framework covers all stages.

### Journey Stage: Awareness (Problem Recognition)

**Buyer mindset:** "I have a problem but I'm not actively looking for a solution."

**Message altitude:** High. Lead with the problem/shift, not the product. Strategic narrative territory.

**Message types:**
- Problem framing (the shift in the world, the stakes)
- Thought leadership (educational content that reframes how they think about the problem)
- Category-level messaging (what kind of solution exists, not which one)

### Journey Stage: Consideration (Solution Exploration)

**Buyer mindset:** "I'm evaluating approaches and building a shortlist."

**Message altitude:** Medium. Lead with differentiated value and benefits. Product messaging territory.

**Message types:**
- Value propositions (why this approach, why this category of solution)
- Benefits (what outcomes they can expect)
- Comparison frameworks (how to evaluate options — set the criteria in your favour)

### Journey Stage: Decision (Vendor Selection)

**Buyer mindset:** "I'm comparing 2-4 finalists and making a final choice."

**Message altitude:** Low. Lead with features, proof, and competitive differentiation. This is where persona-specific and competitive messaging earn their keep.

**Message types:**
- Feature-level detail (capabilities that differentiate)
- Proof points (customer evidence, metrics, case studies)
- Competitive differentiation (why us, not them)
- Objection handlers (addressing specific concerns)
- ROI/business case support

### Journey Stage: Post-Purchase (Adoption & Expansion)

**Buyer mindset:** "I've bought — now I need to succeed and justify my decision."

**Message altitude:** Practical. Lead with enablement and expansion.

**Message types:**
- Onboarding messaging (getting started, quick wins)
- Product education (features they haven't discovered)
- Expansion messaging (additional products, use cases, upsell)
- Customer success stories (validation that they made the right choice)

## 4. Competitive Messaging & Differentiation

Competitive messaging answers the question every prospect asks: **"Why should I choose you over [competitor]?"**

### Building a Competitive Messaging Framework

**Step 1: Identify the competitive set.**

Include:
- Direct competitors (same category, same function)
- Adjacent competitors (different category, overlapping function)
- The status quo (spreadsheets, manual processes, in-house tools, doing nothing)

Remember: 40% of B2B deals are lost to "no decision." The status quo is almost always the most dangerous competitor.

**Step 2: Map your differentiation.**

For each competitor, identify:
- Where you're clearly better (and can prove it)
- Where they're clearly better (and you need to acknowledge it)
- Where you're comparable (and the difference doesn't matter)

**Step 3: Build the differentiation talk track.**

For each major competitor:

```
COMPETITOR: [Name]

Who they are: [1-sentence description]

Their positioning: [How they describe themselves]

Where we win:
1. [Differentiator] — [Why this matters to the customer]
2. [Differentiator] — [Why this matters to the customer]
3. [Differentiator] — [Why this matters to the customer]

Where they win:
1. [Their advantage] — [How to reframe or acknowledge]

When prospects bring them up:
[Suggested response framework — not a script, a framework]

Key proof point:
[The single strongest evidence that we're better for the right customer]
```

### Competitive Messaging Principles

**Never trash-talk.** Disparaging competitors makes you look insecure and unprofessional. Instead, reframe the conversation around what matters for the customer.

**Set the evaluation criteria.** If you can define what the prospect should look for when evaluating solutions — and your criteria favour your strengths — you've already won half the battle.

**Acknowledge trade-offs.** Saying "they're great for X, but we're built for Y" is more credible than claiming superiority across the board. Positioning is about owning a specific place, not claiming every place.

**Lead with differentiated value, not feature checklists.** Feature comparison tables are a race to the bottom. Value differentiation is defensible. "We're the only platform that verifies every single participant" is stronger than "we have 40+ quality checks."

**Use the competitive alternative framing from positioning.** If positioning work identified that the real competitive alternative is "hire an intern and use a spreadsheet," the competitive messaging should address that — not just the named competitors.

## 5. Building Talk Tracks for Sales

Talk tracks translate the messaging framework into conversational patterns sales reps can use naturally. They're not scripts — they're frameworks.

### Elevator Pitch (30 seconds)

Derived from the company value proposition:

```
"We help [target customer] [achieve outcome] by [how]. Unlike [competitive alternative], 
we [key differentiator]. [Proof point]."
```

### Discovery Conversation Starters

Derived from the strategic narrative (the shift):

```
"What we're seeing in [industry/space] is [the shift]. How is that affecting [their role/function]?"
```

This opens a conversation about the prospect's reality rather than launching into a pitch.

### Objection Handlers

For each common objection:

```
OBJECTION: "[The objection as the prospect says it]"

ACKNOWLEDGE: "[Show you understand their concern]"

REFRAME: "[Change the frame of reference]"

EVIDENCE: "[Proof that addresses the concern]"

ADVANCE: "[Next step or question that moves the conversation forward]"
```

### Value Story Template

For use in presentations and demos:

```
SITUATION: [What the customer's world looked like before]
CHALLENGE: [What problem they were facing, in their words]
SOLUTION: [How they used [product], briefly]
OUTCOME: [Measurable results they achieved]
QUOTE: [In their own words, what changed]
```

## 6. The Messaging Matrix

The messaging matrix is the master view that maps all messaging dimensions together. It's a planning tool, not a customer-facing document.

### Structure

```
                    | Persona A        | Persona B        | Persona C        |
                    | (VP Engineering) | (Researcher)     | (Product Manager)|
--------------------|------------------|------------------|------------------|
Company Value Prop  | [Variant]        | [Variant]        | [Variant]        |
Product 1 Headline  | [Variant]        | [Variant]        | [Variant]        |
Product 2 Headline  | [Variant]        | [Variant]        | [Variant]        |
Key Benefit 1       | [Variant]        | [Variant]        | [Variant]        |
Key Benefit 2       | [Variant]        | [Variant]        | [Variant]        |
Proof Point         | [Variant]        | [Variant]        | [Variant]        |
Objection 1         | [Handler]        | [Handler]        | [Handler]        |
```

### When the Matrix Is Worth Building

- 3+ products AND 3+ personas = matrix is essential
- 1 product, 1 persona = matrix is overkill (just build the messaging house)
- Any complex enterprise sale = matrix helps ensure sales teams have the right messages for the right people

### Matrix Quality Check

Every cell should pass the four-point validation (clarity, relevance, value, differentiation). Cells that are forced or generic indicate that either the persona distinction isn't real or the product doesn't have a meaningful variant for that audience.

## 7. Common Pitfalls

**Personas that are demographics, not psychographics.** "Marketing Manager, 28-35, urban" is a demographic profile. "Marketing Manager frustrated by data quality who's been burned by bad vendors and evaluates carefully" is a messaging-relevant persona. The messaging-relevant details are the concerns, fears, and evaluation criteria — not age and location.

**Too many personas.** If you have 8 personas, you have zero actionable personas. Start with the 2-3 roles most involved in the buying decision. Expand only when there's clear evidence that additional personas need distinct messaging.

**Competitive messaging that's a feature checklist.** Feature comparison tables are tedious and easily gameable. Lead with value differentiation and use features only as proof of that value.

**Talk tracks that are scripts.** If sales reps read the talk track verbatim, it sounds robotic and inauthentic. Talk tracks should provide the framework and key phrases, not a word-for-word script. Reps need to internalise the story, not memorise lines.

**Persona messaging without persona research.** Writing messages for a VP of Engineering without ever talking to one produces messaging that sounds like a marketer's guess of what a VP of Engineering cares about. It usually is. Validate persona messaging with real conversations whenever possible.

**Ignoring the status quo as a competitor.** The biggest competitive threat in most B2B sales is inaction. If your competitive messaging only addresses named competitors, you're ignoring the most common reason deals die.

**Building the matrix before the foundation.** The persona/competitive layer only works if the company and product messaging houses are solid. If the core messages are weak, persona variants of weak messages are still weak. Build bottom-up: company → product → persona/competitive.
