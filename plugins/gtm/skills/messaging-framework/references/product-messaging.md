## 1. When You Need Product-Level Messaging

Not everything needs its own messaging house. Apply this filter:

**Needs a product messaging house:**
- Has its own value proposition (distinct from the company value prop)
- Is sold or pitched independently
- Has its own landing page or dedicated marketing
- Has features complex enough to need structured explanation
- Gets questions from sales or support that need consistent answers

**Does NOT need a product messaging house:**
- Is a minor feature within a larger product
- Is only referenced in passing (e.g., in a feature list)
- Doesn't have a buyer who asks about it specifically

### Connection to Brand Architecture

The brand architecture (from the Brand Architecture skill) defines the hierarchy. The product messaging houses follow that hierarchy:

```
Company Messaging House (one)
├── Core Product Messaging House
│   └── Feature-level messaging (within the house)
├── Product A Messaging House
├── Product B Messaging House
└── Service A Messaging House
```

Each product messaging house should reference and reinforce the company-level messaging while adding product-specific detail.

## 2. The Product Messaging House Structure

Follow this template for each product. It pairs a copy-ready messaging house with a benefit hierarchy (section 6).

```
PRODUCT MESSAGING HOUSE: [Product Name]

├── Umbrella Statement (the product's value proposition in a nutshell)
├── Short Product Description (1-2 sentences, more detail than the umbrella)
├── Benefit Hierarchy
│   ├── Aspirational Benefits (why should users care at the business level?)
│   ├── Emotional Benefits (how should users feel?)
│   ├── Functional Benefits (what does the product enable?)
│   └── Functional Attributes (what does the product offer?)
├── Core Messages: Customer Benefits (💙)
│   ├── Benefit 1: [Label]: [Explanation]
│   ├── Benefit 2: [Label]: [Explanation]
│   └── Benefit N: [Label]: [Explanation]
├── Core Messages: Features (👉)
│   ├── Feature 1: [Label]: [Explanation]
│   ├── Feature 2: [Label]: [Explanation]
│   └── Feature N: [Label]: [Explanation]
├── Use Cases (if applicable)
│   ├── Use Case 1
│   ├── Use Case 2
│   └── Use Case N
├── Customer Outcomes (if applicable)
│   └── [Specific outcomes customers achieve]
└── Related Assets
    ├── One-pagers
    ├── Documentation links
    └── Sales enablement materials
```

## 3. Writing Umbrella Statements

The umbrella statement is the **single most important message for the product.** It captures the product's value proposition in one sentence (maximum two). Think of it as the headline if this product had its own billboard.

### Framework

```
[Action verb] + [the outcome/benefit] + with [product name]
```

Or: Start with the customer's desired outcome, then connect it to the product.

### Examples (fictional, Ledgerly)

- Approvals: "Get every invoice approved on time with Ledgerly Approvals"
- Pay: "Pay every supplier in one run with Ledgerly Pay"
- Reconcile: "Close the month in days with automatic reconciliation"
- API: "Automate your payables with the Ledgerly API"

### Quality Criteria

- **One sentence, one idea.** Don't try to say everything.
- **Starts with the customer's outcome** (not the product's features)
- **Specific enough to differentiate** — could a competitor's product use this same statement?
- **Uses the strongest verb available** — "get," "accelerate," "automate" are active; "leverage," "enable," "utilise" are weak
- **No jargon** — a prospect should understand it without context

### Process

1. List the top 3 outcomes customers get from this product
2. Draft an umbrella statement for each outcome
3. Select the one that best captures the product's primary value
4. Test: if a prospect saw only this sentence, would they want to learn more?

## 4. Writing Benefit Messages

Benefits answer: **"Why should a customer choose this product?"** They're the reasons to believe, structured as customer outcomes.

### The Benefit Message Format

Each benefit gets:
- **A short label** (2-5 words, bolded) — scannable in a list
- **An explanation** (2-4 sentences) — provides detail and proof

Use the 💙 indicator (or equivalent) to visually distinguish benefits from features.

### Examples (fictional, Ledgerly Approvals)

```
💙 Nothing sits in an inbox: Every invoice is routed to the right budget owner
   the moment it arrives, with reminders until it's signed off. No chasing, no
   lost PDFs, no late-payment fees.

💙 Audit-ready by default: Every approval is logged with who, when and against
   which budget, so the year-end audit is an export, not a project.
```

### Writing Good Benefits

**Start with the customer outcome, not the feature:**
- Bad: "We have 40+ approval rules" (that's a feature)
- Good: "Every invoice reaches the right person first time" (that's the benefit the rules enable)

**Be specific about the value:**
- Bad: "Faster approvals"
- Good: "The average invoice approved in under two hours"

**Include the proof within the benefit message:**
- "Over a million invoices a month, the average one approved in under two hours" is both a benefit (speed) and proof (a million a month, two hours).

**Answer "so what?":**
Each benefit should answer the customer's implicit "why should I care?" If you can ask "so what?" after reading a benefit and not have an answer, the benefit isn't finished.

### How Many Benefits?

3-5 per product is the sweet spot. More than 6 and they stop being memorable. If you have more than 6, you either have too many or some are features dressed as benefits.

## 5. Writing Feature Descriptions

Features answer: **"What does the product actually do/include?"** They're the concrete capabilities that deliver the benefits.

### The Feature Message Format

Each feature gets:
- **A short label** (2-5 words, bolded) — scannable
- **An explanation** (1-3 sentences) — what it does, concretely

Use the 👉 indicator (or equivalent) to visually distinguish features from benefits.

### Examples (fictional, Ledgerly Approvals)

```
👉 Budget-owner routing: Approval rules by department, amount and supplier send
   each invoice to the right person automatically.

👉 Duplicate detection: Flags invoices that match one already paid, before
   anyone approves them.
```

### Writing Good Features

**Be concrete and specific:**
- Bad: "Advanced routing capabilities"
- Good: "Approval rules by department, amount and supplier"

**Use plain language:**
- Bad: "Leverages proprietary algorithms to optimise invoice workflows"
- Good: "We show you which invoices are waiting, and on whom"

**Connect features to benefits (when not obvious):**
If the connection between a feature and its value isn't self-evident, add a brief "so you can ___" clause.

## 6. The Benefit Hierarchy (Pyramid)

This is a planning tool, not a customer-facing document. Use it to ensure the product messaging covers all altitude levels.

```
                    /\
                   /  \
                  / AS \     ASPIRATIONAL: Why users should care
                 /______\    (business transformation, competitive edge)
                /        \
               /  EMOT.   \  EMOTIONAL: How users should feel
              /____________\  (confident, in control, empowered)
             /              \
            /  FUNCTIONAL    \ FUNCTIONAL BENEFITS: What product enables
           /__________________\  (speed, quality, collaboration)
          /                    \
         /  FUNC. ATTRIBUTES    \ ATTRIBUTES: What product offers
        /________________________\  (features, specs, capabilities)
```

### How to Use the Pyramid

**Step 1:** Fill in from the bottom up. Start with attributes (what does the product have?), then functional benefits (what does that enable?), then emotional (how does that make users feel?), then aspirational (why does this matter at the business/life level?).

**Step 2:** Check that messaging exists at all four levels. Most B2B products have plenty of attributes and functional benefits but are weak on emotional and aspirational messaging.

**Step 3:** Match altitude to context:
- **Top of funnel** (awareness, brand campaigns): Aspirational and emotional
- **Middle of funnel** (consideration, comparison): Functional benefits
- **Bottom of funnel** (decision, sales): Functional benefits + attributes + proof

**Step 4:** For companies with low brand awareness (most startups), lead with functional benefits. Aspirational messaging only works when the audience already understands what you do (Arielle Jackson's ~15% awareness threshold).

### Example (fictional, a presentation tool)

```
Aspirational: Business partners see them as modern, design-forward, tech-savvy.
              Their ideas get noticed for the right reasons.

Emotional:    Inspired ("I have tools to visualize my thinking")
              Informed ("I know what my team is working on")
              Empowered ("I'm able to present more persuasive ideas")

Functional:   Ease of use, beautiful outputs, streamlined collaboration,
              delightful user experience, ease of sharing.

Attributes:   Templates gallery, intuitive editor, real-time collaboration,
              data integrations, presenter mode, sharing options.
```

## 7. Product Pillars

For products with enough complexity, group features and benefits into **3-5 product pillars.** These are the major themes that structure how the product is communicated.

### Examples

**A presentation tool (fictional):** Create | Collaborate | Present
**Ledgerly API (fictional):** Connect | Control | Automate

### How to Develop Product Pillars

1. List all features and benefits
2. Group them by the activity or outcome they support
3. Name each group with a short, action-oriented label
4. Verify: do the groups cover the full product? Is anything missing?
5. Map each pillar to specific benefits and feature areas

### Pillar Output Format

```
Pillar: [Label]
Benefits: [2-3 benefits this pillar delivers]
Feature Areas: [Specific features grouped under this pillar]
```

## 8. Feature Prioritisation for Messaging

Not all features deserve equal messaging investment. Use this framework to prioritise:

```
                    Innovation
                        ↑
              P3        |        P0
           Delighter    |    Platform/Product
                        |       Launch
     Engagement ←───────┼───────→ Acquisition
                        |
              P4        |        P1
           Baseline     |    Differentiator
                        |
                    Parity
```

**P0 — Platform/Product Launch:** New platform or product. Maximum messaging investment across all channels.

**P1 — Differentiator:** Major feature that's truly innovative, solves a major pain point, and is unique. High messaging investment — integrated campaigns, PR, case studies, dedicated content.

**P2 — Unblocker:** Features that were blockers to adoption and bring parity with competitors. Moderate messaging — targeted communication to audiences who were blocked.

**P3 — Delighter:** Features most relevant to current users, solving existing pain in a unique way. Moderate messaging — product updates, in-app, community.

**P4 — Baseline:** Features bringing parity with competitors for existing users. Light messaging — release notes, help centre, support scripts.

### Channel Mapping by Priority

Higher-priority features get broader channel coverage:
- P0-P1: Launch events, PR, case studies, email campaigns, website, in-app, social, blog, release notes, help centre
- P2: Email, website, in-app, social, blog, release notes, help centre
- P3: In-app, email (active users), social, blog, release notes, help centre
- P4: Release notes, help centre, support scripts

## 9. Related Assets and Sales Enablement

Each product messaging house should include or link to:

- **One-pagers:** At-a-glance overview documents for sales and partnerships
- **Documentation:** Links to product docs, help centre articles, API docs
- **Case studies:** Customer success stories relevant to this product
- **Sales materials:** Battle cards, demo scripts, objection handlers
- **Launch assets:** If the product was recently launched, link to launch materials

### Asset Format

```
Related Assets
📖 [Asset type]: [Brief description of what it is and when to use it]
   [Link or file reference]
```

## 10. Common Pitfalls

**Confusing benefits and features:** The most common mistake. Test: does this describe what the product HAS (feature) or what the customer GETS (benefit)? "300+ filters" is a feature. "Find your exact audience in seconds" is a benefit.

**Writing product messaging in isolation from company messaging:** Product messaging should reinforce and extend company-level claims. If the company value prop is about speed and quality, every product messaging house should connect back to those themes.

**One-size-fits-all benefit messages:** "Better data quality" means different things to a researcher and an AI engineer. If the product serves multiple personas, the benefits may need persona-specific variants (see Mode 3: Persona & Competitive Messaging).

**Feature dump without hierarchy:** Listing 20 features in no particular order overwhelms the reader. Group into pillars, prioritise by P0-P4, and lead with the most impactful.

**No umbrella statement:** Jumping straight into benefits and features without an umbrella statement means the reader has to synthesise the value proposition themselves. They won't. Lead with the headline.

**Stale messaging houses:** Product messaging needs to be updated with every significant product change. Build the update process into the product launch checklist.

**Messaging houses that nobody can find:** Store them somewhere central and accessible. Link them from the product team's workspace, the sales playbook, and the content team's resource hub. A wiki page with clear navigation (Notion or similar) works well.
