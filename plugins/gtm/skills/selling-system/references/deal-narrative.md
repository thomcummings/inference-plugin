# Deal narrative — per-opportunity buyer-enablement (Job 3)

Take one live deal and build what wins the room you're not in. The core belief: B2B
deals are decided in internal conversations the seller never attends, so the
highest-leverage artefact is the one the champion *forwards*, not the one the seller
*presents*. This mode is deep, per-deal context — strongest fed a real opportunity,
honest when thin.

## Contents

## Stakeholder map
Name the buying group (Gartner: ~5.4 people on average, and it's the consensus that
kills deals, not the pitch). For each:
```
Player | Role in decision | What they personally stand to gain / lose | What they need to hear | Their likely objection
```
Roles that must be filled: **economic buyer** (controls the money), **champion**
(sells internally for you), **user** (lives with it), **blocker** (security, legal,
finance, a threatened incumbent owner). A map missing the economic buyer or the
blocker is the tell of an under-qualified deal — route back to Job 2.

## The tailored narrative
Bend the positioning spine (shift → stakes → options → bar → answer) to *this* buyer's
job and *this* buyer's language. Tailoring ≠ rewriting: the spine is the positioning's;
what changes is the specific pain, the specific stakes, and the metric that matters to
the economic buyer. Challenger's teach-tailor-take-control applies here.

## The forwardable business case
The artefact the champion sends round internally. It has to survive being read without
you in the room, by a skeptical economic buyer. Structure:
```
- The problem, in the buyer's own words
- The cost of inaction (quantified — what the status quo costs per quarter)
- The proposed change and what specifically improves
- ROI tied to the economic buyer's metric (not the champion's enthusiasm)
- Proof: the most relevant reference customer / result
- What we're asking for next (a single, concrete decision)
```
The discipline: **anchor ROI on the economic buyer's metric.** A champion is excited
about features; the economic buyer cares about the one number they're measured on.
Find that number and build the case on it. If you don't know it, that's the gap to
close before the case is worth writing.

## The mutual action plan
A shared, dated plan from here to a decision — steps, owners (theirs and yours),
dates. Its real job is qualification as much as project management: a buyer who won't
co-author a MAP is telling you the deal isn't real. Use it to test commitment, not
just to track tasks.

## Handling indecision (JOLT)
When the risk is "no decision" rather than a competitor, objection-handling won't
help — the enemy is the buyer's fear of a wrong call. Offer a clear recommendation
(don't just lay out options), limit the choices, and take risk off the table (pilot,
guarantee, phased start). Most stuck deals are indecision, not disagreement.

## Degrading honestly when context is thin
If the deal context is sparse, do not invent stakeholders, metrics, or a critical
event to look complete. Build what the context supports, and explicitly list what's
missing as the questions to answer next — usually: who's the economic buyer, what's
their metric, what's the compelling event, who's the blocker. Naming the gaps is more
useful than a confident fiction.
