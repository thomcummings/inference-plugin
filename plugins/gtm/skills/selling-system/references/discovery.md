# Discovery & qualification engine (Job 2)

Turn ICP + positioning into what to ask and who to qualify in or out. The output has
two halves: a **question bank** and a **qualification rubric**. The rubric is the part
that beats a blank model, because a good one loses you deals on purpose.

## Contents
- The qualification rubric (and how to build real disqualifiers)
- The question bank
- Diagnosis logic — answers → the buyer's real job
- Grading real discovery calls
- The drift watch

## The qualification rubric
Score fit and intent, and — non-negotiable — name disqualifiers.

```
Dimension        | Signal (observable) | Weight | Green / Amber / Red
Fit: firmographic| ...                 | ...    | ...
Fit: trigger     | in-market signal    | ...    | ...
Intent: pain     | quantified impact   | ...    | ...
Intent: authority| economic buyer named| ...    | ...
Intent: timing   | critical event      | ...    | ...
DISQUALIFIERS    | (any one = walk away)
```

**Building real disqualifiers.** This is where most rubrics go soft. A disqualifier
is a condition that, on its own, means *stop* — no budget authority reachable, no
compelling event this cycle, a problem you solve badly, a segment you lose in
(check win-loss). If your rubric has no line that can end a deal, it's a wish list.
Force at least three genuine disqualifiers, sourced from where deals actually go to
die.

**Fit vs intent.** Fit = are they the right *account* (from `icp`). Intent = is there
a *deal* right now. High fit + low intent = nurture, not pursue. Low fit + high intent
= the seductive trap that wastes a quarter. The rubric must separate them.

**Prefer observable actions over stated intentions.** "They said they're interested"
is weak; "they looped in their VP of Finance" is strong. Ties to the `icp`
trigger-event logic — behaviour outranks stated preference.

## The question bank
Organise by stage, and for each question capture *why* and *what good vs bad answers
sound like* — the "why" and the answer-reading are what make it more than a list.

```
Stage | Question | Why we ask (what it reveals) | A good answer sounds like | A bad answer sounds like
```

- **Open on the buyer's world, not your product.** Situation and problem before any
  capability talk.
- **Draw out the implication.** The single highest-leverage move in discovery is the
  SPIN Implication question — make the buyer articulate the cost of the problem
  unsolved. This is what converts a "nice to have" into a funded priority.
- **Listen for the critical event.** No compelling event, no deal this cycle — surface
  it early so you can disqualify on timing.

## Diagnosis logic — answers → the buyer's real job
Discovery isn't data collection; it's diagnosis. Map what you hear to the buyer's real
**job to be done** (Moesta/JTBD), which is usually a layer beneath the stated
requirement. Requirements are what they think they need; the job is the progress they
are trying to make. Sell the job. Provide a small answer→job mapping so the seller can
translate live.

## Grading real discovery calls (assess mode)
When transcripts are available, this is where the skill most beats a blank model — it
can score a real call against the rubric and question bank:
- Which qualification dimensions did the rep actually establish vs assume?
- Did they get the economic buyer and the critical event, or skip them?
- Did they diagnose the job, or take the requirement at face value?
- Where did they pitch before they'd earned it?
Output: a per-call scorecard + the two or three highest-leverage coaching points.
Keep graded calls as worked examples — they're the regression set.

## The drift watch
When win rates diverge from fit scores — you're winning "low-fit" deals or losing
"high-fit" ones — the rubric is stale, not the deals. Re-baseline the criteria against
recent wins. This is the living component; build it in, don't just snapshot.

Lineage: Moesta/JTBD (diagnosis), Rackham/SPIN (implication questions), MEDDICC
(economic buyer / criteria), Winning by Design (critical event), `icp` (fit +
triggers), Inference doctrine (observable over stated, evidence grading).
