# Marketing plan

A marketing plan is the lock-down layer: the document where strategy and brainstorming
stop being optional and become committed, budgeted, owned work. Upstream, ideas are cheap
and revisable — the growth model sketches the engine, the channel map argues priorities,
the bets portfolio evolves weekly. The plan is where that optionality deliberately dies:
this money, these people, these moves, these numbers, this quarter. Its reader is the
person who has to *run* it — so its test is whether someone could open it on a Monday and
know what to do, and open it in week 12 and know whether it worked.

## Where this sits

The plan **consumes** upstream work; it does not redo it. Growth-model defines the
strategic GTM motion — the plan assumes it. Channel-map argues concentration and produces
The Call — the plan inherits it (deviations stated, never silent). Growth-bets maintains
the evolving idea portfolio — the plan *commits* the survivors: a bet that makes the plan
gets budget, an owner, and a stop-loss; the rest stay in the portfolio, not the plan.
Pressure-test gates any big move before it earns a workstream. Downstream, each initiative
that goes to market gets a campaign-brief, and metrics route through the measurement
framework. If none of that upstream work exists, the plan can still be built — gather the
minimum facts honestly, mark it priors-led, and expect the 90-day re-plan to matter more.

Three principles:

1. **A plan is a set of refusals.** Diagnosis always surfaces more than the team can do.
   The plan's value is the cut — deferred, stopped, declined, each with a reason. A plan
   whose workstream count exceeds capacity is a wishlist wearing a plan's clothes.
2. **The goal must be buyable.** Most plans allocate a budget without ever checking whether
   the budget can purchase the goal. The affordability check comes before any allocation —
   and when the arithmetic says no, the plan says no, out loud, and forces the trade-off.
3. **Foundations before amplification.** Attribution, conversion surfaces, positioning and
   proof get fixed before money is poured into traffic. The sequence must show this logic.

## Workflow

### Step 0 — Inventory the upstream work and the hard facts

Read what exists, in priority order: marketing audit (maturity scores, findings),
growth-model output (engine/motion), channel map (especially The Call), growth-bets
portfolio, positioning/messaging, measurement framework. Then establish the three facts
everything reconciles to:

- **The goal** — the business outcome the plan serves, with its number and date, and its
  decomposition basis (current ACV, win rates, funnel conversion, churn — pull real figures;
  every one you can't find is an instrumentation gap the plan must fix).
- **The envelope** — cash/month, plus any one-off pots, plus what's already committed
  (agency retainers, tools) which is spend the plan reallocates or defends, not new money.
- **Capacity** — named people × time, including founder hours and external pens. Time is a
  budget; the plan spends it with the same discipline as cash.

### Step 1 — The affordability check (before any allocation)

Reverse the arithmetic from the goal, on current economics, labelling every step as the
inference it is:

> Goal: +£X ARR by [date] → ≈ N new customers at current ACV (net of churn — if churn is
> unmeasured, say so and bracket it) → at current win rate, ≈ M pilots/opportunities → at
> current funnel conversion, ≈ P demo requests / SQLs over the period → against current
> volume of Q/month. The gap is the plan's real job: [P−Q trajectory].

Then the verdict, one of three, stated plainly:

- **Buyable** — current economics plus the envelope plausibly close the gap. Show which
  workstreams carry which share of it.
- **Buyable with a thesis** — the arithmetic only works if something improves (conversion
  rate, ACV, win rate, a new channel performing). Name the thesis, the workstream that
  owns it, and the early signal that says it's working. Most plans live here; the sin is
  hiding the thesis, not having one.
- **Not buyable** — the envelope cannot purchase the goal on any honest reading. The plan
  refuses to pretend: present the trade-off (smaller goal, bigger envelope, longer
  timeline, or a different motion — which is a growth-model conversation, not a plan) and
  plan to the option the client chooses. Writing a confident plan to an unbuyable goal is
  the most expensive form of politeness.

### Step 2 — Choose the workstreams

Workstreams are objectives, not channels ("Make trials convert", not "Email"). Typically
3–5, never more than capacity can own — one named owner each, and no owner appearing twice
at meaningful allocation without the plan admitting the overload. Each workstream states:
objective, why now (cite diagnosis), the moves inside it (concrete, sized), owner, its
share of the affordability gap, and its one judging metric with baseline → target → date
(route metric choice through the measurement framework where one exists; flag
instrumentation gaps rather than inventing baselines).

### Step 3 — Allocate the budget

Now, and only now, split the envelope. The allocation table shows cash *and* time per
workstream, and classifies every line:

- **Proven** — spend on things with evidence behind them (this client's evidence, not the
  industry's). Should dominate: roughly 70–80% of the envelope in a typical plan.
- **Experimental** — committed bets (from growth-bets or the sprint), each with a
  stop-loss: the amount, the checkpoint date, and the number at which it stops. Experiments
  without stop-losses are proven-spend impostors.
- **Reallocated** — money the stops release, shown moving: "£1,400/mo freed from paid
  search → funds freelancer extension" is a plan; money vanishing into the general pot is
  leakage.
- **Unallocated buffer** — hold 5–10% unassigned. The re-plan will know things this plan
  doesn't; a fully-allocated envelope is a plan with no steering.

State the unlock rules: what evidence promotes an experimental line to proven scale, and
where that money comes from. If committed spend (retainers, tools) survives the plan
unexamined, examine it — inherited spend is where dead budget hides.

### Step 4 — Sequence: the arc and the sprint

Two timescales, deliberately asymmetric in detail. **The 12-month arc**: three or four
horizons at a paragraph each — what each is *for* (fix → build → amplify is the common
shape), what graduates between them, and the evidence that gates each transition. Don't
detail months 4–12; they get re-planned, and pretending otherwise is theatre. **The 90-day
sprint**: full detail — moves per workstream by fortnight, owners, dependencies, quick-wins
woven through, and the stops executed in weeks 1–2 (stops release the capacity and cash the
starts need; schedule them first).

### Step 5 — The refusals register (mandatory)

A visible section: everything this plan does *not* include — deferred initiatives (with
promotion conditions), inherited stops from the channel map, growth-bets that didn't make
the cut this quarter, and wishlist items that didn't survive the affordability check — each
with a reasoned line. This is the section that makes the plan defensible in the room, and
the one the client cannot easily write for themselves.

### Step 6 — Risks and the re-plan

The 3–5 assumptions the plan leans on hardest (the affordability thesis is always one),
each with its break consequence and cheap early signal. Then the re-plan: date (end of
sprint, ~90 days), the data that must exist by then, who convenes it. The arc's next
horizon is written *then*, on evidence.

### Step 7 — Ship

Use `references/template.md` for structure. Label claims — evidence / client-provided /
inference / judgement; the affordability arithmetic is inference and says so. Run the
client voice and quality pass, if you have one, before delivery. British English.
