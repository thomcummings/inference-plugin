# Campaign brief

A brief is a decision-forcing document, not a description. Its job is to make the cheap
arguments happen *before* the expensive work starts: what exactly are we trying to change,
in whose mind, with what single idea, and how will we know. The same rubric that writes a
good brief also reviews a bad one — so this skill has two modes pointed at one standard.

The standard, in one sentence: **one objective, one audience, one proposition, success
defined before launch.** Most campaign failures are visible in the brief as a violation of
one of those four "ones".

Before substantive work in either mode, read `references/examples.md` — a worked good
brief and a worked bad brief annotated with its rubric violations. The bad one matters
more: it fails the way real briefs fail (politely, plausibly, by committee), and the
review register to use is demonstrated in its annotations.

## Mode: CREATE

### Inputs to gather first

The play or initiative itself; any upstream documents (positioning, messaging house, channel
map, marketing plan — a brief *inherits* positioning, it never re-derives it; if no
positioning exists, flag that as a blocking gap rather than improvising one); audience/ICP
material; budget and deadline; who has approval.

### The brief

One page of brief; appendices for everything else. The discipline of the single page is the
point — length is where briefs go to hide their unresolved arguments. ALWAYS use this
structure:

# [Campaign name] — brief
**Status:** draft for argument / agreed · **Owner:** [name] · **Approver:** [name] · **Date:**

## Why this, why now
[2–3 sentences: the diagnosis fact or plan workstream this serves. Cite the source.]

## Objective — the one change
[One measurable change in the world, with a number and a date. "Awareness" is not an
objective; "200 PR agencies request the report by March" is. If the team wants two
objectives, that's two campaigns or one argument that hasn't been had yet.]

## Audience — who, exactly
[Specific enough to picture one person: role, situation, the trigger moment. Include the
insight: what they currently believe/do that the campaign must work with or against.
"E-commerce businesses" is a category, not an audience.]

## Proposition — the single-minded thing
[One sentence they should take away. Drawn from the messaging house. The test: would the
audience member repeat it in their own words to a colleague? Support points (max 3) go
below it, clearly subordinate.]

## Channels & assets
[Where it runs and what gets made — each asset with an owner and a date. Channels must trace
to where the audience actually is (cite the channel map if one exists).]

## Flighting & budget
[Timeline with the launch moment and the rhythm after it; budget split. Campaigns are
moments + echoes, not evenly-spread drizzle.]

## Success, defined now
[The judging metric (one), its baseline and target; 1–2 leading indicators readable in week
one; the guardrail (what we won't sacrifice). Plus the kill/iterate checkpoint: the date and
the number at which we change course. If instrumentation can't read the metric, say so here
and fix it before launch — route to the analyst skill if available.]

## Risks & dependencies
[Max 3, with the cheap early signal for each.]

### Notes on writing it well

- Fight scope creep at the objective line. Every stakeholder addition lands there first.
- The proposition is the hardest line. If it's taking longest, that's correct. If it came
  fast, it's probably a category description wearing a proposition's clothes.
- Budget honesty: state what the team is *not* doing while this runs — campaign capacity
  comes from somewhere.
- Label judgement as judgement, per the practice's claims discipline. British English.

## Mode: REVIEW

Used on a draft brief, a campaign plan, or an in-flight campaign description. Read the
material, then score it against the rubric below — but write the review as prose with
teeth, not a checklist dump. Be kind to the people and blunt about the work.

**The rubric (the four "ones" plus four):**

1. **One objective?** Measurable, dated, singular — or a bundle of hopes?
2. **One audience?** Could you picture the person — or is it a TAM statement?
3. **One proposition?** Single-minded and repeatable — or feature soup / category claim?
4. **Success defined before launch?** Judging metric + baseline + checkpoint — or "we'll
   see how it goes"?
5. **Channel–audience match:** does media placement trace to where this audience actually
   is, or to what the team enjoys making?
6. **Effort–ambition match:** does the budget/asset plan plausibly buy the objective, or is
   it a £500 plan for a £50k outcome (or the reverse)?
7. **Inheritance:** does it use the established positioning/messaging, or quietly invent new
   claims? (Quiet reinvention is how brands fragment.)
8. **The echo plan:** is there life after launch day, or does everything detonate at once
   and go silent?

**The verdict — always end with one of three, plus the three highest-leverage fixes:**

- **Ship** — sound; fixes are polish.
- **Fix first** — the idea survives, but named violations (cite rubric numbers) must be
  resolved before spend. List exactly what "fixed" looks like.
- **Rethink** — the brief fails on objective, audience, or proposition; fixing the others
  won't save it. Say which "one" failed and offer the question whose answer would restart
  it properly.

For in-flight campaigns, add: what the data so far actually says against the (stated or
reconstructed) success definition, and the cheapest change with the largest expected effect
— in-flight reviews should bias to few, decisive adjustments over a re-plan mid-air.
