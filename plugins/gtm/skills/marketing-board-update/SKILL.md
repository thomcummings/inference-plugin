# Marketing board update

Turns marketing performance into a strategic narrative a board will trust, and
returns it ready to present.

The job is not to make marketing *look* strategic. It is to show marketing's real
strategic contribution — honestly and legibly — so leadership can make better
decisions. A board update that dresses up thin performance as strategy is the
fastest way to lose a board's confidence. Honesty about what isn't working, paired
with a clear diagnosis and response, is what earns trust (see *Handling misses*).

## What this skill is for

Preparing the marketing story inside: board decks, investor updates, monthly/
quarterly marketing reviews, fundraising support, CEO/CFO-ready summaries.

This skill owns the **narrative and framing layer**. It sits downstream of the
analysis skills (marketing-audit, growth-model, channel-map, positioning). If the
analysis isn't done, do it first — this skill packages conclusions, it doesn't
manufacture them.

## The core principle

A board does not want a marketing activity log. It wants to know whether marketing
is creating efficient growth, improving revenue quality, reducing commercial risk,
and helping management decide better. So every update answers five questions:

1. How much growth did marketing create?
2. How efficiently did it create it?
3. What did we learn about the market, funnel, or customer?
4. What is changing as a result?
5. What decision, support, or trade-off is needed from leadership?

If a section doesn't help answer one of these, it belongs in an appendix or an
operating review — not the board narrative.

## Workflow

### 1. Establish the frame before writing anything

Confirm four things (ask if they aren't obvious from what's provided):

- **Audience** — full board, investor update email, or an internal exec review?
  A board deck section is tighter and more decision-oriented than a monthly review.
- **Period** — the reporting window, and what the prior period + plan/target were.
  A number with nothing to compare against is rarely persuasive.
- **Stage** — seed, Series A, or Series B. This shifts what the board cares about.
- **GTM motion** — default assumption is **sales-led / pipeline-driven B2B**
  (CAC, payback, pipeline, demo-to-opp). If it's PLG, community/content-led, or a
  marketplace, the vocabulary changes. Read `references/stage-and-motion.md` to
  adjust emphasis and metrics for both stage and motion.

### 2. Sort the raw material into the arc

Reorganise whatever you were given into this sequence — impact first, then
explanation, then forward action:

1. Executive summary (3–5 bullets: the key commercial change, on/off plan, the
   biggest insight, the top risk, the ask).
2. Revenue & pipeline contribution.
3. Efficiency & growth quality.
4. Strategic bets & what was learned.
5. Market insight & risk.
6. Decisions, asks, and next-period priorities.

### 3. Collapse activity into 2–4 strategic bets

This is the move that makes marketing read as strategic rather than busy. Do **not**
present a list of channels or a task log. Group the work into a handful of themes
framed as capital allocation. For each bet:

- **Objective** — the commercial outcome it targets.
- **Hypothesis** — what you believed and why.
- **Actions** — what was done (kept brief).
- **Evidence so far** — including the honest trade-off.
- **Commercial implication** — what it means for revenue quality/efficiency.
- **The call** — scale, iterate, or stop.

**Example — "Move upmarket in messaging":** *Objective:* improve enterprise
demo-to-opportunity conversion. *Hypothesis:* positioning over-indexes on ease-of-use
and under-signals governance and ROI. *Actions:* homepage, case studies, sales
collateral, lifecycle. *Evidence:* higher conversion among target accounts, some
softening in SMB engagement. *Implication:* better fit for ACV goals, with a
possible top-of-funnel trade-off. *The call:* continue in enterprise paths, keep a
lighter message for self-serve. That trade-off — stated, not hidden — is what shows
strategic control.

### 4. Apply the "so what" to every number

After each metric or update, state the implication. A number without an implication
makes management look passive. "Demo volume rose 18%, but conversion quality weakened,
so spend is moving to higher-intent channels" beats "demo volume rose 18%." Lead with
the commercial outcome, never the marketing action.

### 5. Choose the few metrics that matter

Don't dump every metric — more numbers don't buy more credibility. Present in
descending strategic importance (business impact → growth quality → leading
indicators → activity), and keep activity metrics in the appendix unless they
predict a material outcome. Show each headline KPI with current / prior / target /
variance / a one-line read. Full hierarchy and the board-language translations are
in `references/metrics-hierarchy.md` and `references/language-and-reframing.md`.

### 6. Include judgement, not just reporting

The difference between a status report and a strategic update is a stated point of
view: "the evidence now suggests X is a stronger acquisition lever than Y", "the
messaging shift is improving enterprise conversion but lengthening the sales cycle".
Form and defend that view. If the read is genuinely contested or high-stakes,
consider routing it through the `point-of-view` or `pressure-test` skills before
committing it to the update.

### 7. Handle misses honestly

Never bury underperformance — boards value control over spin. Report a miss in five
moves: state it clearly, quantify the gap, explain the likely cause, say what has
changed, give the expected timeline. Example: *"Marketing-sourced pipeline finished
14% below plan, primarily because paid search volume fell after we narrowed keyword
coverage and enterprise webinar conversion dropped. Spend has moved to partner-led
programs and higher-intent search clusters. Recovery is expected over two quarters
if conversion holds."* Diagnosis and response — not excuse-making.

### 8. Keep the main narrative strategic

Keep channel detail, campaign tables, and attribution notes for an appendix so the
main narrative stays strategic. Do **not** produce a slide deck — the end user
reformats into slides to fit their own board template, and that's out of scope.

## Final test

- Does it open with revenue, pipeline, or a strategic business change?
- Does every major item say why it matters commercially?
- Is every metric shown against plan or prior period?
- Is activity grouped into 2–4 strategic bets, not a channel list?
- Is there a clear view on what's working, what isn't, and what changes next?
- Does it contain real risk and judgement, not just reporting?
- Is there a clear ask or decision for the board?

If any answer is no, it's still too tactical — fix it before you present it.
