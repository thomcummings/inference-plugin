# JTBD Interview Guide for Language-Market Fit

This reference helps prepare and conduct Jobs To Be Done interviews specifically optimised for extracting language-market fit data. While JTBD interviews are often used for product development, the focus here is different: capturing the exact words, phrases, and emotional language that customers use, which will become the raw material for customer-facing copy.

## Who to Interview

### Ideal Participants

Interview people who have **recently** made a decision — the fresher the memory, the richer the detail.

**If your product is live:**
- Recent sign-ups (within the last 30 days) — they remember their decision journey
- Recent converts from free to paid — they crossed a commitment threshold and can articulate why
- Customers who switched from a specific competitor — they can articulate what wasn't working

**If your product hasn't launched:**
- People who recently bought a competitor's product
- People who recently adopted a workaround for the problem you solve (spreadsheets, manual processes, cobbled-together tools)
- People who recently asked for "alternatives to [competitor]" on social media, Reddit, or community forums

### How Many

5 interviews is the minimum to start seeing patterns. 8–12 is ideal. Beyond 12, you typically see diminishing returns for language-market fit purposes (though product teams may want more for feature prioritisation).

### Recruiting

Reach out directly via email or in-app message. Frame it as low-stakes research:

"I'm doing some early research to understand the language people use when talking about [category/problem]. It's very informal — more like a documentary than a survey. There are no right or wrong answers. It takes about 20–30 minutes. Would you be up for a quick chat?"

Offering a small incentive (gift card, account credit, swag) increases response rates but isn't essential if you frame the ask well. The key is making them feel like their story matters, not that they're being studied.

## The Interview Framework

### Setting the Tone

Open by establishing that this is casual, exploratory, and that there are no right or wrong answers. You want their story, not their analysis.

Useful opening framing: "Think of this like a documentary. I want to recreate the scene — the moment you first realised you needed something, what you tried, how you decided. The little details that seem unimportant are often the most useful to me."

### The One Core Question

The entire interview can flow from a single prompt:

**"Tell me about how you came to [sign up for / purchase / start using] [product]."**

Then follow the threads. Your job is to help them reconstruct the timeline, pressing for specificity at every point.

### The JTBD Timeline

Guide the conversation through these phases of the customer's journey. You don't need to ask about them in order — follow the story as the interviewee tells it, but make sure you eventually cover all phases.

**First Thought** — the moment they first realised they had a problem or a need. This might have been months before they actually did anything about it. "When did you first think 'I need to do something about this'? What was happening in your life/work at that moment?"

**Passive Looking** — the period where they were aware of the problem but not actively seeking a solution. They might have asked friends casually, noticed ads, or read articles. "Between that first moment and when you started seriously looking — what happened during that time? Did anything keep reminding you of the problem?"

**Trigger Event** — the specific event that shifted them from passive to active looking. This is gold for copywriting because it represents the moment of highest motivation. "What happened that made you think 'OK, I need to actually do something about this now'? Can you take me to that specific day?"

**Active Looking** — how they searched for solutions, what they compared, what criteria mattered. "How did you look for solutions? What did you type into Google? Who did you ask? What options did you consider?"

**Decision** — how they chose, what tipped the balance, what almost stopped them. "What made you choose [product] over the other options? Was there a moment you almost didn't go through with it? What would have made you choose differently?"

### The Language-Extraction Techniques

These techniques are specifically designed to surface the exact customer language needed for LMF copy:

**Unpack vague terms immediately.** When they say "better," "easier," "more flexible," "convenient," or "all-in-one" — interrupt politely: "When you say 'easier,' what specifically do you mean? What was hard about it before?" This is the single most important interviewing technique for language-market fit. Vague words are placeholders for specific experiences. Your job is to dig out the specific experience.

**Push for causality.** When they describe a sequence of events, ask what caused each transition: "You mentioned you'd been frustrated with this for months — what made you actually start looking for a solution on that particular day?" The causal language reveals the triggers that should appear in your copy.

**Listen for emotional energy.** Pay attention to changes in tone — sighs, laughter, raised voice, pauses. When you hear emotional energy, stop and explore: "Wait, tell me more about that. Why was that moment so important?" Emotional language is the highest-converting copy material.

**Use the "describe it to a friend" probe.** When you want their natural vocabulary (not their analytical vocabulary), ask: "If you were telling a friend about this over coffee, how would you describe what [product] does for you?" This tends to produce the most natural, resonant language.

**Play back the story wrong.** Intentionally misstate a detail: "So you said you looked at about three options before deciding?" when they said five. People naturally correct you, and the correction often reveals what details matter most to them and prompts them to elaborate.

**Use analogies to unlock stuck moments.** When they can't find the words, help with analogies: "Is it kind of like [analogy]?" They'll usually refine it: "No, it's more like…" — and that refinement is often perfectly worded copy.

**The "two-minute pitch" question.** Near the end: "If you had two minutes to convince a friend to try [product], what would you say?" This produces remarkably usable headline-ready language because they're naturally condensing their experience into the most persuasive framing.

### Questions Bank

Below are questions organised by interview phase. Don't use all of them — pick the ones relevant to your context and let the conversation flow naturally. The core question ("Tell me about how you came to...") should carry most of the interview, with these as probes when you need to dig deeper.

**Uncovering the Struggle:**
- What were you trying to do when you first realised you needed something new?
- What was the hardest part of [the task your product helps with]?
- What were you using before? What didn't work about it?
- When you think back to before [product], what was the most frustrating part of your day/week?
- Can you describe a specific moment when [the old way] really let you down?

**Uncovering the Goal:**
- What does success look like for you in [area]?
- If [product] worked perfectly, what would your day/week look like?
- What would being great at [task] let you do that you can't do now?
- What did you hope would change when you started looking for a solution?

**Uncovering the Decision Journey:**
- Walk me through how you looked for solutions. What did you search for? Where did you look?
- What other options did you consider? What made you rule them out?
- Was there a moment you almost didn't sign up? What nearly stopped you?
- What did [product] say or show you that made you think "this might work"?

**Uncovering Social/Emotional Dimensions:**
- How did this problem affect your relationship with your team/boss/clients?
- Did you feel any anxiety about making this choice? What were you worried about?
- How did it feel when [product] actually worked? What changed for you emotionally?
- Has anyone else noticed the change since you started using [product]?

**Closing Questions:**
- If you had to explain what [product] does to a friend in one sentence, what would you say?
- What's the one thing about [product] that matters most to you?
- Is there anything I haven't asked about that was important in your decision?

## Processing the Transcripts

### Recording and Transcription

Always record interviews (with permission). Use a transcription service or AI tool to generate written transcripts. Don't rely on notes alone — you need exact words, not your paraphrase of their words.

### The Language Mining Process

After completing all interviews, go through each transcript and extract:

1. **Verb clusters** — Pull out every verb the customer uses when describing their struggles and goals. Group them by frequency. The most common verbs, and their surrounding context, are your headline raw material. (This is exactly how Mindstone's CEO generated their 10× headline — text-mining interview transcripts for frequent verbs.)

2. **Struggle statements** — Every sentence where the customer describes what was wrong, hard, frustrating, or broken. Capture verbatim.

3. **Goal statements** — Every sentence where the customer describes what they wanted, hoped for, or were trying to achieve. Capture verbatim.

4. **Trigger descriptions** — Every sentence describing what finally made them act. Capture verbatim.

5. **Outcome descriptions** — Every sentence describing how things changed after adopting the solution. Capture verbatim.

6. **Emotionally charged phrases** — Any moment with strong emotion (frustration, relief, excitement, anxiety). These are the highest-signal phrases for copy.

7. **Natural comparisons** — How they describe the product relative to alternatives. "It's like [X] but for [Y]" statements are positioning gold.

### Organising the Output

Create a structured language bank with four columns:

| Category | Exact Quote | Frequency | Emotional Intensity |
|----------|-------------|-----------|-------------------|
| Struggle | "I was spending half my Friday doing this manually" | 4/8 interviews | High (sighing) |
| Goal | "I just want to know who's working on what" | 6/8 interviews | Medium |
| Trigger | "When we lost that client because nobody followed up" | 2/8 interviews | Very high |
| Outcome | "Now my Monday starts with a clear picture" | 3/8 interviews | High (relief) |

Rank entries by frequency × emotional intensity. The top entries are your primary copy candidates.

Hand this language bank to Mode 2 (Message Craft) as input for copy generation.

## Practical Tips

- Schedule interviews for 30 minutes but be prepared to go 45 if the conversation is rich
- Morning interviews tend to produce better recall than afternoon ones
- If doing interviews over video call, ask the participant to be somewhere they can talk openly (not in an open office)
- Interview in pairs if possible — one person leads the conversation, the other takes notes on body language and emotional cues
- Leave a 15-minute gap between interviews to write down your immediate impressions while they're fresh
- After every 3–4 interviews, do a quick pattern check — are themes emerging? If the same phrases keep appearing, you may be reaching saturation
- Don't be afraid to say "I'm confused" — it's one of the most powerful prompts because it gives the interviewee permission to elaborate without feeling judged
