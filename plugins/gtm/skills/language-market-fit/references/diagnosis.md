# Language-Market Fit Diagnosis

This reference guides the assessment of existing copy against language-market fit principles. Use it when the user provides a website URL, landing page, ad copy, app store listing, pitch deck, or any customer-facing text and wants to know whether they have language-market fit.

## Input Requirements

You need at minimum:
- The copy to assess (ideally the full page, not just the headline)
- What the product/service does
- Who the target customer is

Helpful but not essential:
- Current conversion rates (to benchmark against LMF ranges)
- Any existing customer research or interview data
- Competitor landing pages for comparison
- The company's stage (pre-launch, seed, Series A, etc.)

## The Assessment Framework

Score the copy across seven dimensions. Each dimension gets a score of 1–5 and a finding with specific evidence.

### Dimension 1: Customer-Centricity (weight: high)

**What to evaluate:** Does the copy centre the customer's world — their goals, struggles, anxieties, desired outcomes — or the company's world — features, capabilities, team credentials, technology?

**Scoring rubric:**
- **5:** Headline and subheadline are entirely about the customer's goals/struggles. Feature language only appears after the value is established. Reader thinks "that's exactly my problem."
- **4:** Headline is customer-centric. Some feature language creeps into subheadlines but is tied to outcomes.
- **3:** Mixed — some customer language, some company language. Reader has to work to connect features to their life.
- **2:** Predominantly company-centric. Features and capabilities lead. Customer benefits are implied, not stated.
- **1:** Entirely company-centric. "We are…" "Our platform…" "We help…" No evidence of customer language.

**The quick test:** Does the headline complete "Now you can ___" (customer-centric) or "Our product is ___" (company-centric)?

### Dimension 2: Specificity (weight: high)

**What to evaluate:** Does the copy use specific, concrete language about what the customer can achieve, or does it rely on vague platitudes?

**Red flag words and phrases:** "all-in-one," "seamless," "powerful," "revolutionary," "cutting-edge," "best-in-class," "next-generation," "world-class," "robust," "streamline," "leverage," "empower," "end-to-end," "scalable," "flexible," "convenient," "faster," "better," "easier." These words force prospects to guess what you actually do.

**Scoring rubric:**
- **5:** Language is highly specific. A reader could explain to a friend exactly what this product does and why it matters after a 5-second scan. Concrete tasks, outcomes, or scenarios are named.
- **4:** Mostly specific with one or two vague terms that could be tightened.
- **3:** A mix of specific and vague. The core offering is somewhat clear but surrounded by filler.
- **2:** Predominantly vague. Heavy use of platitudes. Reader must already know the category to understand the value.
- **1:** Almost entirely abstract. Could describe any SaaS product. "A powerful all-in-one platform that helps teams work better."

### Dimension 3: Comprehension Speed (weight: high)

**What to evaluate:** Would a prospect understand the core value proposition within 5 seconds of scanning (not reading) the page? This maps to the "information foraging" model — the lizard brain needs to pattern-match instantly.

**Scoring rubric:**
- **5:** Headline alone conveys what you help the customer do. No ambiguity. Works without subheadline.
- **4:** Headline plus subheadline together make it clear within 5 seconds.
- **3:** Need to read the full above-the-fold content. Some scanning and interpretation required.
- **2:** Need to scroll below the fold to understand what the product actually does.
- **1:** Even after reading the full page, unclear what specific problem this solves for whom.

**How to simulate the 5-second test:** Read only the headline and subheadline. Could you explain to someone what this product does and why they might want it? If you're reaching or inferring, it fails.

### Dimension 4: Struggle/Goal Resonance (weight: medium)

**What to evaluate:** Does the copy surface real, specific struggles and goals that the target customer would recognise from their own experience? Does it trigger the "that's me" recognition?

**Scoring rubric:**
- **5:** Copy names specific, emotionally resonant struggles or goals. Uses first-person framing or "you" language that mirrors how customers talk about their situation. E.g., "I hate asking people for a copy of their passports."
- **4:** References recognisable situations but language is slightly polished or abstracted from how customers would actually say it.
- **3:** Mentions the general problem space but doesn't name specific struggles or goals.
- **2:** Implies a struggle exists but focuses on the solution rather than the felt experience.
- **1:** No evidence of customer struggles or goals. Pure feature/capability language.

### Dimension 5: Differentiation Clarity (weight: medium)

**What to evaluate:** Could a prospect distinguish this product from competitors after reading the copy? This isn't about claiming superiority — it's about whether the language creates a clear mental model of what this specific product does differently.

**Scoring rubric:**
- **5:** The copy creates a unique, memorable mental model. You couldn't swap in a competitor's name and have it still make sense.
- **4:** Largely differentiated. One or two lines could apply to competitors but the overall message is distinct.
- **3:** Somewhat generic to the category. A competitor in the same space could use similar copy.
- **2:** Very generic. Could describe any of 5+ competitors in the category.
- **1:** Complete commodity language. Could describe any SaaS product in any category.

### Dimension 6: Proof and Trust (weight: low-medium)

**What to evaluate:** Does the copy include social proof, specificity of outcomes, or trust signals that reduce the perceived risk of engaging further? This is less about language-market fit specifically, but weak proof undermines even strong language.

**Scoring rubric:**
- **5:** Specific, quantified proof points. Named customers with concrete outcomes. Credible trust signals.
- **4:** Good social proof present but could be more specific or prominent.
- **3:** Generic social proof ("Trusted by 1,000+ companies") without specificity.
- **2:** Minimal proof. Maybe a logo bar but no testimonials or outcomes.
- **1:** No social proof at all.

### Dimension 7: Call-to-Action Alignment (weight: low)

**What to evaluate:** Does the primary CTA match the level of intent the copy is likely to generate? Copy targeting low-intent visitors should lower the barrier (free trial, see a demo, learn more). Copy targeting high-intent should match urgency (start now, get started, buy today).

**Scoring rubric:**
- **5:** CTA perfectly matches the intent level and follows naturally from the value proposition.
- **4:** CTA is reasonable but could be better aligned to the copy's emotional arc.
- **3:** CTA is generic ("Get started") — functional but not optimised.
- **2:** CTA mismatch — asks for high commitment from low-intent traffic.
- **1:** CTA is confusing, missing, or actively counterproductive.

## Producing the Assessment Output

### Summary Score

Calculate a weighted score:
- Dimensions 1–3 (high weight): multiply by 3
- Dimensions 4–5 (medium weight): multiply by 2
- Dimensions 6–7 (low-medium/low weight): multiply by 1

Maximum possible: (3×15) + (2×10) + (1×10) = 75
Express as a percentage and map to a band:

- **70–100% (Strong LMF):** Language-market fit is largely achieved. Focus on testing variants and optimising.
- **45–69% (Partial LMF):** Some elements work but significant gaps exist. Targeted rewrites will help.
- **25–44% (Weak LMF):** Fundamental language-market fit issues. Needs a ground-up rewrite informed by customer research.
- **0–24% (No LMF):** Copy is disconnected from customer reality. Before rewriting, gather voice-of-customer data. Route to Mode 3 (Interview Preparation).

### Report Structure

Present the assessment as:

1. **Overall verdict** — one-sentence summary and band (Strong / Partial / Weak / No LMF)
2. **Score breakdown** — each dimension with score, evidence, and a specific finding
3. **The headline test** — apply the "Now you can ___" vs "Our product is ___" test to the actual headline and show which it is
4. **Red flags** — list any platitudes, vague terms, or company-centric framing found, with exact quotes
5. **Quick wins** — 2–3 specific rewrite suggestions that would have the highest impact, using the principles from the main SKILL.md
6. **Root cause assessment** — is this a language problem (knows the customer, just using wrong words) or a positioning problem (doesn't yet understand what customers actually care about)? If positioning, recommend going upstream.
7. **Recommended next step** — either specific rewrites (if Partial/Strong), a full message-craft exercise (if Weak), or customer research (if No LMF). Route to the appropriate mode.

### Rewrite Principles

When suggesting specific rewrites:
- Flip "Our product is…" statements into "Now you can…" framings
- Replace platitudes with specific tasks, outcomes, or scenarios
- Lead with the customer's struggle or goal, not the product's capability
- Use first-person struggle framing ("I" not "you") when talking about pain points
- Avoid rhetorical questions — they feel accusatory. State the struggle directly.
- If the product serves a broad audience, find the specific angle that resonates broadly (like "I hate asking people for a copy of their passports" for compliance software)
- Check Google's "people also searched for" to find the actual vocabulary customers use, not the vocabulary the company uses internally

### When to Flag Upstream Issues

During diagnosis, watch for these signals that the problem is positioning, not language:
- The copy is clear and specific but describes a value proposition that doesn't match what customers actually care about
- The product tries to appeal to too many different audiences with different needs
- There's no clear "job" the product is hired to do
- The competitive landscape makes it hard to differentiate on any specific dimension
- The team can't articulate in simple language what one specific customer can now do that they couldn't before

In these cases, note the language-market fit score but recommend a positioning exercise before further copy work. If the positioning skill is available, suggest they start there.
