# Message Craft: Generating Copy with Language-Market Fit

This reference guides the generation of customer-facing copy from voice-of-customer data. Use it when the user has customer research inputs and needs headlines, subheadlines, landing page copy, ad copy, or messaging frameworks that achieve language-market fit.

## Input Tiers

The quality of output is directly proportional to the quality of input. Determine which tier of input the user has and set expectations accordingly.

### Tier 1: JTBD Interview Transcripts (Gold Standard)
Raw or lightly edited transcripts from Jobs To Be Done interviews with recent customers. This is the best possible input because it contains the exact words customers use to describe their struggles, goals, and decision journey.

**What to extract:** Verbs (the most revealing part of speech for goals and struggles), emotionally charged phrases, specific scenarios described, the language of "first thought" moments, and the words customers use when describing what they were trying to do before they found the product.

### Tier 2: Voice-of-Customer Data (Strong)
Customer survey responses, support tickets, review text (G2, Capterra, app store reviews), sales call notes, onboarding questionnaire answers, social media comments, community forum posts, or "cancel reason" data.

**What to extract:** Recurring phrases, the specific words customers use (not your paraphrase of them), emotional language, comparisons to alternatives, and descriptions of the "before state" (life without the product).

### Tier 3: Customer Description + Positioning (Adequate)
A clear description of the target customer, their situation, key struggles, desired outcomes, and existing positioning work (e.g., a positioning canvas). No raw customer language available.

**What to extract:** Inferred struggles and goals, the "job" the product is hired to do, and the competitive alternatives customers are coming from.

### Tier 4: Product Description Only (Minimum Viable)
Just a description of what the product does. No customer research.

**What to do:** Flag that this tier produces the weakest language-market fit. The output will be educated guesses, not customer-validated language. Strongly recommend gathering primary VoC data (route to Mode 3) and treat the output as a starting draft to validate, not a final answer. Still produce useful output — just set expectations clearly.

## The Extraction Process

Before writing any copy, mine the inputs for raw material. This is the most important step.

### Step 1: Build the Language Bank

Go through the input material and extract entries into four categories:

**Struggles** — what the customer was frustrated by, stuck on, or suffering from before finding a solution. Look for phrases starting with "I was tired of…" "I hated…" "It drove me crazy that…" "I couldn't figure out how to…" "Every time I tried to…" "The problem was…"

**Goals** — what the customer was trying to achieve. Look for: "I needed to…" "I wanted to…" "I was looking for a way to…" "I wished I could…" "If only I could…"

**Trigger moments** — the specific event or situation that made them start actively looking for a solution. This is the "first thought" in JTBD terms. Look for: "The moment I realised…" "When [specific event] happened…" "My boss asked me to…" "We lost a deal because…"

**Outcome language** — how they describe life after finding the solution. Look for: "Now I can…" "Finally…" "It's like having…" "I no longer have to…" "For the first time…"

For each entry, capture the exact words — don't paraphrase. Note the frequency of specific phrases across multiple sources. Phrases that appear independently from multiple customers are highest-signal.

### Step 2: Identify the Dominant Job

From the language bank, identify the primary "job" customers are hiring the product to do. Express it in the JTBD format: "When [situation], I want to [goal], so I can [outcome]."

There may be multiple jobs. Rank them by frequency in the data. The most common job should drive the primary headline. Secondary jobs can inform supporting copy.

### Step 3: Find the Emotional Core

Beyond the functional job, look for the emotional and social dimensions. What do customers feel before (frustrated, anxious, overwhelmed, embarrassed) and after (confident, relieved, in control, proud)? The emotional dimension often differentiates good copy from great copy.

Look especially for social contexts: "My team thinks I'm…" "My boss expects…" "I look like I don't know what I'm doing…" "I want to be seen as…" These social anxieties and aspirations are powerful copy fuel.

## The Copy Generation Process

### Headlines

Generate 5–8 headline variants in three categories:

**Goal headlines** — complete the sentence "Now you can ___." Use the most common goal language from the language bank. These work best when prospects are aware of what they want but haven't found the right solution.

Examples of the pattern:
- "Build a custom ad server in just weeks." (Kevel)
- "Create a website you're proud of." (Wix)
- "Organize, share and take notes on web pages, PDFs and academic papers easily, so you can learn faster, remember more and get things done." (Mindstone — the 10X winner)

**Struggle headlines** — state the struggle in first person ("I" not "you"). These work best when prospects are aware of the problem but reluctant to act, or when the struggle is more emotionally resonant than the goal. Follow with a goal-oriented subheadline.

Important: Use "I" framing, not "you" framing. "I'm tired of getting screwed by my bank" works. "Are you tired of getting screwed by your bank?" feels aggressive and accusatory.

**Trigger headlines** — reference the specific moment or situation that prompts action. These work well for paid search and retargeting where you can match the trigger context. "Just been told your audit is in 3 months?" is more powerful than "Prepare for your next audit."

### Subheadlines

For each headline, write a complementary subheadline that:
- If the headline is a goal → the subheadline adds specificity about how or adds a proof point
- If the headline is a struggle → the subheadline pivots to the goal/solution ("Now you can…")
- If the headline is a trigger → the subheadline names the outcome that resolves the trigger

### Supporting Copy Blocks

Generate 3–4 supporting sections for a landing page or key messaging document:

**The "before/after" block** — contrast the customer's life before and after, using their actual language. This is where struggle and outcome language combine. Avoid features. Focus on felt experience.

**The "how it works" block** — 3 steps maximum. Each step should be framed as what the customer does or experiences, not what the product does technically. "Upload your document" not "Our AI-powered NLP engine processes your document."

**The "proof" block** — take any outcome language from customers and frame it as social proof. Customer quotes should be lightly edited for clarity but preserve their original vocabulary. The most powerful testimonials describe the before/after in the customer's own words.

**The "objection" block** — address the top 1–2 anxieties or doubts surfaced in the research. If customers mentioned concerns about switching cost, learning curve, data security, or price, address them directly. "I was worried about [concern]. Here's why that turned out not to be an issue."

### Keyword Cross-Reference

After generating copy, do a search volume sanity check. Sometimes the vocabulary customers use in interviews doesn't match what they type into Google. For example, customers might say "stock trading" in interviews but search for "investing app" (which has 2× the search volume). When there's a mismatch, note it and suggest A/B testing both vocabulary sets.

Check Google's "People also searched for" and autocomplete suggestions for the primary terms. These reveal the actual search vocabulary in the prospect's head during goal-directed information foraging.

## Output Formats

Depending on the user's request, produce one of these outputs:

### Option A: Headline Test Set
A set of 5–8 headline/subheadline pairs, categorised by type (goal/struggle/trigger), with a note on which customer language each was derived from and a recommended testing plan (which pairs to A/B test first, using ad impressions rather than landing page tests for speed).

### Option B: Landing Page Copy
Full above-the-fold copy (headline, subheadline, CTA) plus 3–4 supporting sections. Structured as a content brief that a designer could implement. Include the hierarchy: what the visitor should understand at 5 seconds (headline), 15 seconds (headline + subheadline + CTA), and 60 seconds (full above-fold + first scroll).

### Option C: Messaging Framework
A reference document the team can use across channels. Includes:
- Primary value proposition (one sentence)
- 3 headline variants per message type (goal/struggle/trigger)
- Key phrases from the language bank, tagged by source
- Words to use / words to avoid (based on customer vocabulary vs. company jargon)
- Channel-specific guidance (landing page, paid search, social, email, sales enablement)

### Option D: Ad Copy Set
Short-form variants optimised for paid channels. For each, specify the target awareness level (problem-aware, solution-aware, product-aware) and the recommended channel. Follow the same goal/struggle/trigger framework but compress to character limits.

## Comprehension Validation Plan

After generating copy, always include a recommended comprehension testing protocol:

1. Show the headline to someone for 5 seconds, then take it away (no warning)
2. Ask: "Do you recall what that said?" (Tests readability — did they actually parse the words?)
3. Ask: "What do you think that is? What would it do for you?" (Tests comprehension — do they understand?)
4. If they parrot the headline back: "But what do you think that means?" (Tests depth — is it meaningful?)
5. Repeat 4× per headline variant
6. Iterate based on comprehension failures

This must be done with real humans. AI cannot substitute for the 5-second comprehension test because the test is about the human attention/foraging process, not about whether the words are logical.

## Common Failure Modes to Watch For

When generating copy, actively avoid these patterns:

- **The curse of knowledge:** Using internal jargon, category names, or technical terms that the team uses daily but customers don't. "CI/CD pipeline" vs. "ship code without breaking things."
- **Feature creep in headlines:** Trying to mention too many things. One clear message beats three compressed ones.
- **Aspirational overreach:** "Transform how your team works" when the customer just wants to "stop losing track of what everyone's working on."
- **Social proof that doesn't prove anything:** "Trusted by 10,000+ companies" is less powerful than "Reduced Sarah's audit prep from 3 weeks to 2 days."
- **Generic SaaS voice:** The copy sounds like it was written by a SaaS copywriting template. If you could swap in any competitor's product name and the copy still works, it's too generic.
- **"You" accusation framing:** "Are you struggling with X?" feels aggressive. "I'm struggling with X" feels empathetic. When referencing struggles, prefer first-person or third-person framing.
