# Language-Market Fit

Language-market fit is when you find the exact right words to explain your product or service to prospective customers — words that resonate with goals and struggles already in their brains. When you achieve it, prospects feel like you've read their minds.

This skill is based on Matt Lerner's language-market fit framework (First Round Review / SYSTM), grounded in Jobs To Be Done methodology and attentional control neuroscience.

## Core Principles

These principles apply across all three modes of this skill. Internalise them before doing any work.

### 1. Prospects are foraging, not reading

People don't read websites like books. They scan, doing a rapid pre-conscious pattern-match against a target already in their heads — their goal, struggle, or desired outcome. Psychologists call this "information foraging." Your copy needs to "look like food" to pass this triage. You have nanoseconds, not minutes.

### 2. The words are already in their heads

The right copy isn't invented by clever copywriters — it's discovered in customers' own language. The best-converting headlines use the exact words customers use to describe their struggles, goals, and desired outcomes. Text-mining interview transcripts and pulling out the most common verbs and surrounding context is a proven technique.

### 3. The "Now you can ___" test

If a headline completes "Our product is…" — it's company-centric and will underperform. If it completes "Now you can ___" or "I wish I could ___" or "Someday I hope to ___" — it's customer-centric and has a much higher chance of resonating. Every headline should pass this test.

### 4. Specificity beats breadth

Vague platitudes ("all-in-one solution," "faster," "revolutionary," "convenient") engender cynicism and force prospects to guess what you do. Specific goals and tasks resonate even with broad audiences. "I hate asking people for a copy of their passports" beat generic compliance messaging across fintech, RE, HR, and legal audiences.

### 5. Functional before aspirational

Nike and Apple earned ephemeral, allusive messaging over decades of built awareness. Unknown startups need clear functional messaging first. Even Nike started with "A shoe for the serious runner" and the iPhone launched with feature-focused ads. Start functional, earn aspirational.

### 6. Language-market fit is upstream of everything

It's not a marketing tactic — it's the "lead domino." Clear, validated language helps you understand your market, decide what to build, validate demand, demonstrate traction, align your team, and improve every aspect of the customer experience. If you don't have it, everything else is harder than it needs to be.

## Three Modes

This skill operates in three modes. Determine which the user needs based on their request, or ask if unclear.

### Mode 1: Diagnosis (Assessment/Audit)

**When to use:** The user has existing copy (website, landing page, ad, app store listing, pitch deck) and wants to know whether they have language-market fit and what to fix.

**What to do:** Read `references/diagnosis.md` and follow the assessment framework. This produces a scored audit with specific findings and rewrite recommendations.

**Typical triggers:** "audit my copy," "why isn't my landing page converting," "review my messaging," "does this resonate," "what's wrong with our website copy," "assess our language-market fit"

### Mode 2: Message Craft (Copy Generation)

**When to use:** The user has customer research data (JTBD interview transcripts, survey responses, customer quotes, support tickets, review mining data) or at minimum a clear description of their target customer's goals and struggles, and wants to generate copy that achieves language-market fit.

**What to do:** Read `references/message-craft.md` and follow the generation process. This produces headline variants, subheadlines, supporting copy blocks, and a comprehension testing plan.

**Typical triggers:** "write copy from these interviews," "turn this customer research into headlines," "write my landing page," "what should my headline say," "help me find my language-market fit," "write messaging from this VoC data"

### Mode 3: Interview Preparation

**When to use:** The user doesn't have customer research yet and needs to gather voice-of-customer data before they can find language-market fit. This is the "step zero" path.

**What to do:** Read `references/interview-guide.md` and help them prepare a JTBD interview script, identify who to interview, and set up a transcript analysis framework.

**Typical triggers:** "I don't have customer interviews yet," "how do I gather the language," "help me prepare customer research," "JTBD interview questions," "I need to talk to customers first"

## Relationship to Positioning

If the user has completed a positioning exercise (e.g., with the positioning skill), language-market fit sits downstream. Positioning answers "what are we, who is it for, and why should they care?" LMF answers "what exact words do we use so their brains light up?"

During diagnosis, if the copy is clear but the value proposition itself doesn't resonate, flag that the problem may be upstream in positioning rather than in language.

## Important Caveats

- Language-market fit is less critical for: biotech/pharma with obvious clinical need, founders building for their own exact use case, or products with zero competition in a well-defined category.
- The framework assumes B2B or B2C products with a digital acquisition funnel. It's most powerful for startups with low brand awareness competing for attention.
- AI can help draft and iterate copy, but the gold-standard input is real customer language from real interviews. Encourage the user to gather primary VoC data whenever possible.
- The "5-second test" for comprehension validation should be done with real humans, not simulated. Offer to help design the test protocol but don't pretend AI validation substitutes for it.
