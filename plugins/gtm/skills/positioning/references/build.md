# Build: the six moves

Build or rebuild positioning from evidence. Work the moves in order, since each one depends
on the one before. Grade every line Seen, Said or Supposed as you go.

## Before you start

- **Evidence.** Ask for whatever exists: win/loss notes, call recordings, interview
  transcripts, reviews, support threads, usage data and the CRM's closed-lost reasons. If
  there's none, run the Build on informed views and grade the lines Supposed.
- **People.** Ideally someone from product, someone from sales, someone from success and
  someone who can make the call. With a single stakeholder, label the result a draft.
- **Permission to let go.** Agree up front that the original idea for the product is not
  binding. Ask: "If you had to explain this product to someone who'd never heard of the
  category you started in, what would you say?" The answer is often closer to the truth
  than the current homepage.

Common signs that the founding category is getting in the way:
- "We're an X with a special feature" when the special feature is why people buy.
- The market is named after where the idea came from, not where the value lands.
- The team keeps a category because it's familiar, not because buyers search for it.

## Move 1: Proof set

**Question:** Which customers chose us fastest and got the most from us?

Ask for 5 to 10 named accounts that:
- understood the product quickly and bought without a long fight,
- use it heavily or have expanded,
- would recommend it, or already have,
- look like the customers you want more of.

Add three to five recent losses where the buyer was a good fit on paper. Losses show what
the product isn't winning on.

For each account, write one line on what they were doing before and one line on why they
chose you, in their words if you have them. **Reconstruct two or three wins in detail.**
For each, cover the trigger that started the search, who was involved, what else they
considered, the moment they decided and what tipped it. Most of the canvas comes out of
these reconstructions.

**Output:** the proof set, with a before and a why for each, and two or three
reconstructed wins.

## Move 2: Fallbacks

**Question:** If we didn't exist, what would these customers have done instead?

List what the proof set would realistically have used, not every company in the space.
Fallbacks come in four kinds:

- **Direct:** products that solve the same problem in a similar way.
- **Different approach:** products from another category that solve it another way, such
  as an agency, a consultant or a general-purpose platform.
- **Do it by hand:** spreadsheets, email, a shared inbox, an extra hire, a script someone
  wrote.
- **Carry on as now:** decide the problem isn't worth solving this year.

Two traps:
- **Rivals you never meet.** Companies that could compete in theory but never show up in
  deals. Leave them out, because they blur the positioning.
- **Only direct rivals.** If every fallback is a lookalike product, ask "what were they
  using before they found us?" The answer is usually do it by hand, or carry on as now.

**Output:** 3 to 5 fallbacks ranked by how often they show up, with at least one of do it
by hand or carry on as now.

## Move 3: Edges

**Question:** What can we do that each fallback can't?

List everything that's genuinely different, without judging its worth yet: features,
architecture, data nobody else has, the pricing or delivery model, expertise, integrations,
community and partnerships. Then tie each edge to the fallback or fallbacks it beats. An
edge is only an edge against something.

**Look again at the "weaknesses".** Something that counts against you with one buyer can be
the reason another buyer chooses you. A tool that takes a week to set up loses to a free app
for a two-person team, and beats it for a finance team that needs approval rules for every
cost centre.

For each edge, ask whether it will last. Could a fallback copy it within a year?

**Output:** a table of edges, the fallbacks each one beats, and how durable each one is.

## Move 4: Payoffs

**Question:** What is each edge worth to the customer?

Buyers don't buy edges. They buy what the edges let them do. Translate each edge into an
outcome, then group the outcomes into two or three payoffs.

**Translation chain (fictional example, Ledgerly accounts-payable software):**
- Edge: approvals route to the budget owner automatically, based on the cost centre.
- What it lets them do: invoices stop waiting in someone's inbox.
- Outcome: suppliers are paid on time, and month-end close takes days instead of weeks.

**Grouping:** Ledgerly's edges might group into two payoffs. "Nothing waits on a person"
covers routing, reminders and mobile approval. "The books close themselves" covers
automatic matching, ledger sync and the audit trail.

Each payoff needs an outcome you could measure, and proof from the proof set. A payoff with
no proof is graded Supposed.

**Output:** 2 to 3 payoffs, each with the edges behind it, an outcome and proof.

## Move 5: Frame

**Question:** What reference point makes these payoffs obvious to the people who'd value
them?

The frame is the category or comparison a buyer files you under. It sets their
expectations about features, rivals and price before you've said anything else. Choose the
frame that makes your payoffs look obvious and makes your gaps look irrelevant.

There are three options, in order of cost and risk:

1. **Borrow a frame buyers already use.** "Accounts-payable software." Buyers already know
   what it means and are already searching for it. You stand out within it. This is the
   default, and it's right for most companies.
2. **Narrow a known frame.** "Accounts-payable software for multi-entity finance teams."
   The frame tells the right buyers it's for them, shrinks the set of rivals and makes your
   edges obvious. Check that the narrow frame is big enough to grow in, or has a clear next
   step out of it.
3. **Name a new frame.** Only when no existing frame describes what the product does, and
   you have the money, time and voice to teach the market what the new frame means. It's
   the most expensive option and usually takes years. Many companies later celebrated for
   creating a category started by competing in an existing one.

Ask the user to say what the chosen frame leads buyers to expect: features, rivals and
price. If those expectations work against the product, the frame is wrong.

**Riding a trend (optional).** Tie the frame to a trend only when your best-fit buyers
already care about it and the product genuinely delivers on it. A trend the buyers don't
care about is just noise.

**Output:** the chosen frame, which option it is, why, what it leads buyers to expect, and
any trend tie-in.

## Move 6: Fit

**Question:** Who feels these payoffs most, and could we build a list of them?

You're describing the observable traits of an account that values your payoffs more than
most, not a persona. Use the proof set as the starting evidence.

- **Firmographic traits:** size, industry, region, stage, business model.
- **Situation traits:** tools they already use, how the team is structured, how mature the
  function is, regulation they face.
- **Triggers:** the event that starts a search. That might be a new hire, a failed audit, a
  merger, a funding round or a switch of tools.

Tighter is better. "Finance teams at multi-entity companies with 200 to 1,000 staff, on
NetSuite, closing more than three entities a month" is far more useful than "mid-market
finance teams".

**The list test:** could marketing build a target-account list from this description today,
and could sales build an outbound list from it? If not, get more specific.

Note who is not a fit, and why. It helps sales disqualify early.

**Output:** 4 to 6 observable traits, the triggers, the champion's role, and who isn't a
fit.

## Give something up

Name what this positioning stops claiming. Good positioning chooses, so it should make some
buyers, some use cases and some messages less of a priority. Ask: "Which deals would we now
walk away from? Which homepage lines would go? Which feature stops being something we lead
with?" If the answer is "nothing", the positioning hasn't made a choice yet.

## Stress-test it

| Test | Pass if |
|---|---|
| **Swap test** | A named rival couldn't say the same position truthfully |
| **Fallback test** | It beats the top fallback, including carry on as now, not just the direct rivals |
| **Sixty-second test** | Three people from different teams explain it in a minute and say roughly the same thing |
| **Customer-words test** | It sounds like how the proof set describes you, not how the team does |
| **List test** | Marketing and sales could build target lists from the fit description |
| **Expectation test** | What the frame leads buyers to expect matches what the product is good at |
| **Evidence test** | No payoff rests only on Supposed lines without a plan to prove it |

When two moves pull against each other, for example a frame that hides your strongest
payoff, say so. That tension is often where the real insight is.

## Spread it

Once the positioning is agreed, hand it to the downstream work: the messaging house
(`messaging-framework`), the narrative spine (Story mode), the homepage, sales enablement,
campaign targeting and onboarding for new hires. Set a date to revisit it.

## Facilitating it live

For each move:
1. Explain the move in two or three sentences, and why it comes where it does.
2. Ask the move's question, and push for examples from the proof set.
3. Play back what you heard, with a grade on each line.
4. Flag gaps and contradictions with earlier moves.
5. Move on.

Keep pulling the conversation back to the proof set. Drifting into "our customers in
general" is the most common way positioning goes soft.
