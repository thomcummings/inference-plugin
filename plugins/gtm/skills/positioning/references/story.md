# Story: from positioning to a narrative spine

Turn a settled positioning canvas into the narrative spine that sales, the website and the
deck all borrow from. The spine is built around one idea: **the buyer's hardest job is
choosing with confidence.** Most B2B buyers are buying this kind of product for the first
time, several people have to agree, and the person championing it is personally exposed if
it goes wrong. A story that only pushes the product leaves them on their own at the moment
they need help, which is one reason so many deals end with no decision.

So the spine first helps the buyer understand the choice, and only then shows the product.

## Why familiar pitch shapes fall short

- **Feature tour.** It shows what the product does and leaves the buyer to work out why it
  matters and how it compares.
- **Problem, then product.** Rivals describe the same problem, so nothing sets you apart.
- **Old way versus new way.** There's usually more than one new way, and new isn't the same
  as better.
- **Customer as hero.** It's engaging, but it doesn't help anyone compare options.

None of these shapes is built for the decision itself.

## The spine

Seven beats. The first four set up the choice. The last three answer it. Each beat comes
from a part of the canvas.

| Beat | What it does | Comes from |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Shift** | What has changed in the buyer's world that makes this worth solving now | Frame, triggers, payoffs |
| **2. Stakes** | What it costs to get this wrong or leave it | Payoffs, carry on as now |
| **3. Options** | A fair map of the ways companies handle it today, with the trade-offs | Fallbacks, grouped into approaches |
| **4. Bar** | What a good choice has to do, agreed with the buyer before the product appears | Payoffs, phrased as requirements |
| **5. Answer** | How the product clears each part of the bar | Edges, mapped to payoffs |
| **6. Proof** | Evidence for each part of the bar, from customers like this one | Proof on each payoff |
| **7. Next step** | One specific thing to do next, or an honest "this isn't for you" | Fit, not a fit |

### 1. Shift

Name a change the buyer may not have connected to their problem yet. It should lead
naturally to your payoffs, not to any solution in the category. Write it as: what's
changed, what it breaks, and what most teams haven't noticed yet.

*Ledgerly (fictional):* "Finance teams are being asked to close faster with the same
headcount, while invoices now arrive from more places than ever: email, portals and
procurement tools. The bottleneck isn't keying in invoices anymore. It's getting a busy
budget owner to approve them."

Run the swap test here too. If a rival could open with the same shift, sharpen it.

### 2. Stakes

Make the cost of getting it wrong or leaving it concrete: late-payment fees, supplier
relationships, a slipped close, audit findings. Use the buyer's numbers where you can, and
don't exaggerate. This beat is what turns carry on as now into a real option the buyer
has to weigh.

### 3. Options

Group the fallbacks into two to four approaches and describe each one fairly: what it's good
at and where it breaks. Don't name rivals or knock them, because describing them fairly is
what earns trust. Always include do it by hand or carry on as now. The trade-offs should
lead naturally to your payoffs.

*Ledgerly:* "Most teams handle this one of three ways. They chase approvals by email and
spreadsheet, which costs nothing until volume grows. They use the approval module in their
ERP, which is solid but rigid and lives where budget owners never log in. Or they buy a full
procurement suite, which is powerful but takes months to roll out and is more than many
teams need."

Use this beat for discovery too: ask which approach they use today and what they've
already tried.

### 4. Bar

Before showing the product, agree with the buyer what a good choice has to do. Phrase each
payoff as a requirement, not a feature. Then ask whether it matches how they see it, and
adjust if it doesn't. When the buyer agrees the bar in their own words, the rest of the
story is showing that you clear it.

*Ledgerly:* "So whatever you choose has to get approvals out of people's inboxes without
any chasing, match payments to the ledger without a spreadsheet step, and go live in weeks
rather than a quarter. Is that the right list?"

### 5. Answer

Give a short introduction in the chosen frame, no more than a minute: "[Product] is [frame]
for [fit]." Then go through the bar one requirement at a time. For each, remind them of the
requirement, show the edge that meets it and say what it means for them. In a demo, follow
the bar, not the menu. Leave out features every option has, unless they ask.

### 6. Proof

For each requirement, give the strongest evidence from a customer who looks like this buyer.
That could be a result, a quote, a reference call or third-party validation. Use real
numbers with sources, and never invented ones. Where proof is thin, say so and offer a
pilot or a reference call instead.

### 7. Next step

Ask for one specific step that fits where the deal is: a technical session with their IT
lead next week, not "thoughts?". If the conversation has shown they aren't a fit, say so,
and point them to the option that suits them better. It saves both sides time, and buyers
remember it.

Handle common objections such as switching cost, integration, security, adoption and price
inside the beat they belong to, and only the ones that really come up. Per-deal tailoring,
buying-group maps and business cases belong to `selling-system`.

```markdown

For: [fit description] · From canvas version: [v] · Date: [date]

## Set up the choice
### Shift
[two to four sentences]
Check-in question: [question]

### Stakes
[cost of getting it wrong or leaving it, in the buyer's terms]

### Options
| Approach | Good at | Breaks when |
|---|---|---|
| | | |
| Carry on as now / do it by hand | | |
Discovery questions: [questions]

### Bar
A good choice has to:
1. [requirement → payoff 1]
2. [requirement → payoff 2]
3. [requirement → payoff 3]
Agreement question: [question]

## Answer the choice
### Introduction
[Product] is [frame] for [fit]. [One sentence on what it does.]

### Clearing the bar
| Requirement | Edge that meets it | What it means for them | Demo moment |
|---|---|---|---|

### Proof
| Requirement | Evidence | Source | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|

### Objections that really come up
| Objection | Beat it belongs to | Response |
|---|---|---|

### Next step
Ask: [specific step and timing]
Walk away if: [signals that it isn't a fit]
```

## Keeping it honest

If the spine is hard to write, the canvas usually has a gap, so go back to Build rather
than forcing the story. Build the spine with sales and success, run it in real calls,
and note where buyers lean in, where they drift and which objections come up. Then
revise it.
