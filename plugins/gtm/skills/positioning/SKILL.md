# Positioning

Positioning is the frame a buyer uses to make sense of a product: what it is, what it is
better than, who it is for and why that matters. Buyers always position a product
somehow. The job is to choose the frame on purpose, from evidence, so the product's real
strengths look obvious to the buyers who care about them most.

Positioning is the input to messaging, brand, the pitch, the website and targeting. It is
not a tagline, a messaging document, a brand identity or a positioning statement. A
statement is one way to write positioning down. It is not a way to work it out.

## The method in one view

The Inference method runs six moves, and every line it produces carries an evidence grade.

| Move | Question | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Proof set | Which customers chose us fastest and got the most from us? | 5 to 10 named accounts, plus a handful of losses |
| 2. Fallbacks | What would those customers have done if we didn't exist? | 3 to 5 real fallbacks, at least one of them "carry on as now" |
| 3. Edges | What can we do that each fallback can't? | Edges, each tied to the fallback it beats |
| 4. Payoffs | What is each edge worth to the customer? | 2 to 3 payoffs, each with an outcome and proof |
| 5. Frame | What reference point makes those payoffs obvious? | A frame (borrowed, narrowed or new) and why |
| 6. Fit | Who feels the payoffs most, and can we find them? | Observable traits and triggers that build a list |

Then two closing steps: **give something up** (name what this positioning deliberately
stops claiming) and **stress-test** it (see `references/build.md`).

The order matters. Fallbacks come before edges, because an edge only exists relative to a
fallback. Payoffs come before the frame, because the frame's job is to make the payoffs
obvious. Fit comes last because, once you know what the product is best at, the question
becomes who values that most and how you find them. The proof set from move 1 is your
first answer to that, which move 6 sharpens.

### Evidence grades

Every line on the canvas is marked with one of three grades:

- **Seen:** observed in behaviour. Won and lost deals, usage data, what customers
  actually switched from.
- **Said:** customers said it in interviews, reviews, calls or support threads.
- **Supposed:** the team believes it, but no customer evidence backs it yet.

A canvas where every line is Supposed is a hypothesis, which is fine to start from. Say so
on the canvas, and list the cheapest way to move each Supposed line to Said or Seen.

## Modes

Decide which mode the user needs before you load a reference. Load only that one.

| Mode | Use when | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| **Check** | They're unsure whether positioning is the problem, or need the case for doing the work | `references/check.md` |
| **Build** | They're ready to build or rebuild positioning | `references/build.md` |
| **Story** | Positioning is settled and they need the narrative spine for sales and the website | `references/story.md` |

Routing:

1. Broad "help with our positioning" for a named product → Check, then Build.
2. "We need to do our positioning" → Build.
3. Settled positioning, needs a pitch or narrative → Story.
4. End to end → Check → Build → Story.
5. A concept or definition question → answer from this file.

## Baseline context

Before any mode, gather:

- What the product does, in the user's current words.
- Who buys it today, and who the happiest customers are.
- What is and isn't working in go-to-market right now.
- Any earlier positioning work, and why the need has come up now.
- What evidence exists: win/loss notes, interview transcripts, reviews, usage data.

## Principles

- **Start from the customers who chose you fastest.** Average customers include people
  who bought on a discount, barely use the product or would leave tomorrow. The proof set
  shows what the product is actually best at.
- **The real competitor is often carrying on as now.** Many deals end with no purchase at
  all. Treat "do nothing" and "keep the spreadsheet" as fallbacks in their own right.
- **Describe the product as it is now.** The founding idea is not the product. Let go of
  the category it was conceived in if that category now hides its strengths.
- **Positioning involves the whole team.** Product, sales, success and leadership each see
  different evidence. If you only have one stakeholder, label the output a draft.
- **Positioning goes stale.** Revisit it when the product, the market or the best-fit
  customers change, or when growth stalls without an obvious reason.
- **A frame is a promise about expectations.** Choose the frame that makes your strengths
  obvious and your gaps irrelevant, not the one that sounds the most ambitious.

## Outputs

- **Check:** a positioning check (symptoms, problem type, timing, next steps, evidence
  gaps). Template in `references/check.md`.
- **Build:** a positioning canvas (the six moves, what you're giving up, a one-paragraph
  position, stress-test results and evidence grades). Template in `references/build.md`.
- **Story:** a narrative spine (shift, stakes, options, bar, answer, proof, next step).
  Template in `references/story.md`.

## When information is thin

1. Name which moves lack evidence.
2. Give a working answer anyway, graded Supposed. A rough answer forces the right
   question, so don't skip a move.
3. Name the research that would fill the gap: which interviews, which win/loss review,
   which data pull.

## Downstream

- `messaging-framework` turns the canvas into the messaging house.
- `language-market-fit` finds the exact words the best-fit buyers use.
- `selling-system` uses the narrative spine for qualification and deal narratives.
- `brand-architecture` and `brand-archetype` take the canvas as an input.

## Method lineage

The Inference positioning method is our own structure and wording, built from running
positioning work with B2B software companies. It draws on a long line of positioning
thinking, and two influences in particular deserve credit:

- **April Dunford**, *Obviously Awesome* (2019) and *Sales Pitch* (2023). Her work shaped
  how we think about positioning as context-setting, about competitive alternatives
  including the status quo, and about a sales story that helps the buyer choose between
  approaches. Read both books for the definitive treatment of her method.
- **Al Ries and Jack Trout**, *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind* (1981), for the idea
  that positioning happens in the buyer's mind and that a frame is chosen, not declared.
