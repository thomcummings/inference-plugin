# Point of View

Positioning says what you are, for whom and against what. Brand foundations say what you stand for.
Neither tells the team what to keep *saying* week after week. This skill fills that gap with three
things: one belief about where the market is heading, a few storylines that carry it, and the
subject areas that prove them.

```
BELIEF  →  STORYLINES  →  PROOF AREAS  →  STARTER ANGLES
one bet     3-4 things      subjects you     first pieces, across
about the   you want said   return to        formats, handed to
market      back to you     again and again  the content team
```

Every piece of content should trace back up this chain. An idea that can't is either off-strategy
or a sign a storyline is missing.

## Where it sits

- **Inputs:** positioning (differentiated value, best-fit customers) and brand-architecture
  (strategic narrative, pillars). Read them first if they exist. If they don't, run on what's
  available and say what's missing.
- **Outputs:** messaging-framework takes the durable claims each storyline implies. The content
  team takes the proof areas and starter angles as first briefs.
- **Versus brand-architecture's strategic narrative:** that is *one* company story about a shift in
  the world, changed rarely and used for fundraising, recruiting and the company story. This skill
  produces a market belief for the buying conversation plus a *set* of storylines that content
  carries. If the user wants the single company story, use brand-architecture.
- **Versus content strategy:** keyword research, calendars, formats and distribution belong to the
  content team. This skill stops at the handoff.

## How to work

Ask up front which mode suits the user:

- **Workshop:** take the founder or a small group through the questions live and capture their
  words. Best when the convictions sit in people's heads.
- **Draft:** write a first version from existing positioning, brand and content for the user to
  react to. Faster, and usually right when working from existing material.

Either way the output is a first version to test, not the final word. Storylines only work if the
people who have to repeat them believe them.

Route by what the user asks for:

1. "What do we believe about where this market is going?" → Step 1.
2. "What should we be known for this year? What are our key themes?" → Step 2.
3. "What should our content be about? What do we keep writing about?" → Step 3.
4. "Build the whole thing" → Steps 1, 2, 3 in order.

## Step 1 — The belief

A point of view is a bet about what good looks like in your market a few years from now, made
from the vantage point of what you do best.

**It is not product vision.** Product vision describes what your product will become, and it's
for your team and investors. Buyers pay for the product that exists today. A big vision on a thin
product gives a hesitant buyer a reason to wait. A belief about the *market*, tied to what you can
deliver now, gives them a reason to move.

**It should favour you.** The strongest beliefs are not neutral forecasts. They describe a future
in which your particular strength is what matters most. A company that owns the documents and
inboxes argues that context will matter more than models. A workflow company argues that
governance will decide who wins. That isn't cynical; it's what makes the belief credible coming
from you and hollow coming from a competitor.

**Why buyers care.** In a changing market, uncertainty makes buyers delay. They want someone's
honest read on where things are going, and reassurance they won't regret the purchase in a few
years. A clear belief gives them both, and explains why you build what you build.

**Method**

1. List what you do better than any alternative. Pull it from positioning if it exists.
2. For each strength, finish the sentence: "If this is what really matters, then in three to five
   years the good options in this market will ___."
3. Name the common assumption you're betting against. A belief that contradicts no one is too
   safe to be useful.
4. Write the belief in one or two plain sentences.
5. Check it against the tests below and revise.
6. Connect it to the roadmap. The belief should make your product choices look obvious.

**Tests.** The belief passes when it:

- describes the future of the market, not of your product;
- rests on a strength you can back up, so a competitor saying it would sound hollow;
- is contestable: a serious competitor would believe something different;
- explains your roadmap choices to a buyer;
- reassures a buyer about the next few years, not only today.

**Common failures:** it's really product vision ("the platform for everything"); it's too safe to
argue with ("AI will matter"); it isn't backed by a real strength; or it names a trend with no
"and therefore us".

```
THE BELIEF

Belief:            [one or two sentences on where the market is going]
Rests on:          [the strength that makes it credibly yours]
Betting against:   [the common assumption it contradicts]
Contrast:          [what the main alternatives implicitly believe instead]
Explains:          [the product choices it makes obvious]
Tests:             [pass / what to fix]
```

## Step 2 — Storylines

Storylines are the three or four things you want buyers, customers and the market to think and
say about you this year. They turn one belief into a few repeatable stories, keep content on a
small number of themes, and give everyone in the company the same few things to say.

Each storyline takes a side. A neutral statement of fact won't guide anything.

**Where storylines come from.** A good set usually draws on more than one of these:

- **The company:** why it exists, what it stands for, what it wants to be remembered for.
- **The market:** the Step 1 belief, in a form the audience can repeat.
- **The product:** what's different about it and why it wins.

**Gathering the raw material.** In workshop mode, put these to the founder and whoever owns brand,
product marketing and customers. In draft mode, answer them from existing material and mark what
needs confirming. Capture raw answers; don't polish yet.

- *Convictions:* What do the founders argue about with peers? What would the world look like if
  you won?
- *Identity:* What should people remember you for? What won't you do, even if it would sell?
- *Product:* What do happy customers say about it unprompted? What finally tips a buyer over?
- *Market:* What shift are you part of? Why does this company make sense now and not five years
  ago?
- *Customers:* What do your best customers have in common? Complete: "Teams that [do X] tend to
  pick [company] because ___."

**Shaping.** One person, usually the brand or content lead, groups the answers into three or four
rough themes. This is synthesis by one owner, not a committee vote. Then write each theme as a
sentence the *audience* would say, for example: "If I'm looking at [competitor], I should look at
[company] too," or "With [shift] happening, our current setup won't last." The test for each: is
this the sentence you want repeated?

**Tests.** The set passes when:

- there are three or four, each short enough for anyone in the company to repeat;
- the combination is yours: no competitor could claim the same set;
- most of the year's content (outside pure search content) fits under one of them;
- you can quickly picture several pieces, across formats, for each one. If you can't, the
  storyline is too thin.

**What drives each storyline.** Content alone rarely moves what people think. For each storyline,
note one thing to fix or build (the site's competitor page, the sales deck, onboarding) and one
thing to run (an event, a switching campaign, a partner programme).

```
STORYLINES

Parent belief: [from Step 1]

1. [Storyline, in the audience's words]   (company / market / product)
   Why us, why now: [one line]
   Fix or build: [...] · Run: [...]
2. ...
3. ...
(4.) ...

Tests: [repeatable] [combination ours] [most content fits] [several pieces each]
```

If the founder can't take part, label the output "first version, to confirm with the founder". The
convictions and identity answers are the hardest to substitute, so flag them as missing rather than
inventing a company's beliefs.

## Step 3 — Proof areas and starter angles

A proof area is a subject you can credibly own and keep coming back to, and that backs up a
storyline. It's narrower than the storyline and broader than any single piece.

**A good proof area is:**

- **yours:** rooted in the belief and your strengths, so you can say things others can't;
- **narrow enough to own:** not "the future of work", but a defensible slice of it;
- **broad enough to last:** enough for many pieces over a year;
- **something buyers care about:** your best-fit customers would seek it out.

Aim for two to four per storyline. More suggests the storyline is doing too much.

**Starter angles.** Under each proof area, sketch a handful of content angles across formats:
written, video, audio, events, talks, original data, teardowns. The aim is range, not a finished
calendar. For each angle, take a quick read on four things: how uniquely *you* can say it, how much
buyers care, how directly it backs the storyline, and what it costs to make well. Favour the
unique, wanted and on-point. Don't chase search volume here; that's the content team's job, and
chasing it here dilutes the throughline.

**The handoff.** Say plainly where this skill stops:

- **To messaging-framework:** the lasting claims and proof points each storyline implies, the
  things the site and sales must say consistently.
- **To the content team:** proof areas and starter angles as first briefs. They own the calendar,
  formats, SEO, production and distribution.

Don't produce calendars, cadences, channel plans or SEO targets. If the user asks for them, hand
over this map as the input.

```
POINT OF VIEW — CONTENT HANDOFF

Belief: [...]

Storyline 1: [...]
  Proof area A: [...]
    Angles: [angle] (written) · [angle] (video) · [angle] (event) ...
  Proof area B: [...]
  Fix or build / run: [...]

Storyline 2: [...]
  ...

HANDOFF
  → messaging-framework: [lasting claims and proof points per storyline]
  → content team: [proof areas and angles as first briefs]
```

If the storylines aren't final, build proof areas against the first version and say so. Trying to
find proof areas is a good test of a storyline: one you can't find ownable subjects for is usually
too thin, so send it back to Step 2 rather than forcing it.

## When inputs are missing

This work often runs ahead of finished positioning. When it does:

1. Name which positioning or brand inputs are missing.
2. Give working answers, labelled as hypotheses to test.
3. Say who should weigh in on which call: founders, product marketing, content, a customer-facing
   voice.
4. Surface forks. If different strengths point to different beliefs, show the choice rather than
   averaging it into mush. A belief that annoys no competitor is too weak.

## Context to gather first

- What does the company do, and what does it do better than anyone?
- What positioning, brand foundations or strategic narrative already exist?
- What do the founders have strong opinions about? What shift are they part of?
- What content exists today, and does it have a throughline?
- Who's available to take part?
