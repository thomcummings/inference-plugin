# Phase 1 — Foundation (the floor)

The structural rigour everything else stands on. Skip it and the media-shape overlay
(Phases 2–3) has nothing to hold onto — you get personalities posting into the void.
This phase produces two things: the **content pyramid** (the hierarchy of authority)
and the **funnel × segment × medium mapping**. Together they are the gate against
random acts of marketing.

## 1. The content pyramid

```
   Brand narrative / Point of view     ← input from positioning + point-of-view
        │
   Signature Topics (3–4/yr)        ← the standing claims you want to own
        │
   Content Anchors                     ← the definitive destination per topic
        │
   Regular pieces                      ← evergreen web that feeds & collates up
        │
   Fragments                           ← short-form atoms; distribute AND build authority
   ───────────────────────────
   + Help / hygiene content            ← always-on product & support surface (runs alongside)
```

Each level ladders to the one above. The discipline is vertical traceability: any
fragment should trace to a piece, to an anchor, to a topic, to the narrative. The
hygiene layer (docs, help content, support SEO) runs alongside on its own logic — it
serves existing users and captures bottom-funnel search; don't force it to ladder to a
signature topic, but don't let it masquerade as thought leadership either.

## 2. Deriving signature topics

Signature topics are the hinge between strategy and content — the handoff point from
`point-of-view`. They are the 3–4 big, universal claims the brand wants to own this
year, each reducible to one substantive point of view.

Derive them from: the point of view / market belief, the storylines (the storylines
you want repeated), positioning (the differentiated value and best-fit customer), and
the distinct strength only this brand can credibly claim.

The tests (a topic set passes all four):
- **Few and bold.** 3–4, not 8. Each is universal enough to sustain a year of content
  and specific enough that a competitor's set would look different.
- **Ownable as a set.** The *combination* is claimable by this brand and no rival. Any
  single topic might be shared; the portfolio should not be.
- **Comms-wide.** Each aligns not just the blog but PR, social, email, sales, events. If
  a topic only makes sense for the blog, it's a content theme, not a signature topic.
- **~90% ladder.** Roughly nine in ten non-hygiene pieces this year can trace to one of
  them. If big swathes of planned content don't fit, either the content is drifting or
  the topics are wrong.

When positioning/POV are missing, propose a *working* topic set from whatever clarity
exists and label it a hypothesis to validate with founders — don't present it as fact.

## 3. Choosing content anchors (and why an anchor ≠ an SEO page)

An anchor is the **definitive destination for a topic** — the thing you point everything
else at and return to. In the 2020 playbook "anchor" was fused with "SEO pillar page",
because search was the obvious way to build a hub. Decouple them. The hierarchy of
authority is real; the SEO expression of it is optional.

An anchor can be:
- a **report / index** (an annual state-of, a benchmark study)
- a **recurring show** (a podcast, a video series)
- a **tool** (a calculator, a template, an interactive diagnostic)
- a **course / guide** (a definitive multi-part resource)
- a **definitive essay** (the canonical POV piece on the topic)
- a **pillar page** (still valid where buyers search the topic — one option, not the default)

Choose anchors **brand-first, then sharpen with demand data.** Start from what the brand
must own to prove the topic; then use keyword and AEO/GEO research (hand to
`content-strategy` for depth) to shape the angle, the title, and whether a
search-capturing pillar is worth building alongside the brand-led hub. An anchor earns
its cost by the size of the spider-web it can seed and the credibility it concentrates —
not by search volume alone.

Rule of thumb: 1–2 anchors per signature topic. More and the topic has no centre of
gravity.

## 4. Regular pieces: the spider-web

Regular pieces are the evergreen web around each anchor. They do three jobs: **feed** the
anchor (drive attention to it), **collate up** to it (the anchor links out to and is
built from them — the way Intercom's Jobs-to-be-Done books collate a body of posts), and
**span the funnel** (each piece aimed at a stage and segment). They are where breadth and
discoverability live. Most should be evergreen; a minority can be timely if they still
advance a topic and survive a week-from-now test.

## 5. Fragments and the production-order inversion

Fragments are the atoms — clips, quotes, carousels, hooks, one-liners. In the old model
they were exhaust: you wrote the long thing, then chopped it up. In the 2026 model the
order often **inverts** — short-form is where authority is *built* (through personality
and consistency at the speed of the feed), and the long anchor is the occasional monument
that consolidates it. Two consequences:

- Fragments are not just distribution; they are primary content that earns attention in
  its own right. They still ladder — a fragment carries a signature topic even at
  100 characters.
- Design fragments to be *native*, not amputated. A carousel is not a screenshot of an
  essay; a clip is not a link. The moat gate (Phase 2) applies to fragments too — a
  generic quote-card is slop.

## 6. The funnel × segment × medium mapping

A piece is defined by **where** (funnel stage), **for whom** (segment) and **on what
surface** (medium), not just its topic. Map deliberately. Defaults to adapt:

| Funnel stage | Job | Typical formats | Typical media |
|---|---|---|---|
| Awareness | earn attention, plant the POV | POV essay, podcast, social-native, talk, fragment | social feeds, YouTube, podcasts, events |
| Consideration | teach, differentiate | how-to, framework, comparison, webinar | search, newsletter, LinkedIn, site |
| User / Activation | help them succeed | product-discovery content, docs-as-content, tutorials | site, in-product, email, YouTube |
| Buyer / Decision | de-risk the purchase | case study, ROI content, sales enablement | sales, site, email, direct |

Two disciplines:
- **Cross with the segment.** The same stage looks different for a technical champion vs
  an economic buyer. Aim each piece at a persona from the ICP, or it sprays.
- **Treat the funnel as a lens, not a conveyor.** The real journey is non-linear and
  dark-social — people meet you through a clip, lurk for months, then arrive "ready".
  The map ensures coverage across stages; it does not claim a tidy staircase.

## 7. The placement test (the anti–random-acts gate)

Before any piece enters the model, it must pass:

1. **Ladder** — it traces to an anchor → signature topic → narrative.
2. **Aim** — it has a funnel stage and a named segment.
3. **Job** — it advances an OKR or storyline (Phase 4 makes this explicit).

A piece that fails is either cut, or parked until it can be reshaped to pass. "It'll do
well on LinkedIn" is not a pass. This test is what separates a content model from a
content brand costume.

## 8. Worked example (abbreviated)

*Narrative:* "Marketing is being rebuilt from the ground up for the AI era."
*Signature topic (1 of 3):* **AI-native marketing** — what good marketing looks like
when built AI-native, not retrofitted.
*Anchor:* a definitive essay + living index, "What an AI-native marketing function
actually looks like" (brand-led hub; a search-capturing pillar version tracked
separately if the keyword warrants).
*Regular pieces:* "Rebuilding the content layer", "Research as an agentic workflow",
"The layers that matter more, not less" — each mapped to a stage (mostly
awareness/consideration) and a segment (senior in-house marketers vs founders).
*Fragments:* clips from the podcast episode on the topic, a carousel of the layer-cake
model, three one-line POV posts.
Each item passes the placement test: ladders to the topic, aimed at a stage+segment,
carries a job. Anything that doesn't — e.g. a generic "5 AI tools" listicle — fails and
is cut.
