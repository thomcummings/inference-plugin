# Essence Craft — The Seven Craft Tests

The gates that get applied in Phase 3 (cull) and re-applied in Phase 4 (develop) and Phase 5 (stress). These are *craft* tests, not strategy tests. Strategy tests catch positioning failures; craft tests catch dead-on-arrival writing.

A candidate is a survivor only if it passes — at minimum — **inevitability, claimed word, and one of (music | layer | surprise)**. Generative power and mouth test are applied later in Phase 4.

---

## 1. Inevitability

**The question.** Does it sound discovered, not constructed?

Great essences feel like they were always there waiting to be found. *Belong Anywhere* doesn't feel like the output of a brainstorm — it feels like Brian Chesky said it and the rest of the team went *yes, of course.* That is the bar.

**The tell of constructed work.** Words assembled from a thesaurus or a category-vocabulary palette. Phrases that feel optimised against three things at once. Phrases that bear visible signs of compromise — the give-and-take of a committee that wanted to please everyone.

**The tell of inevitable work.** It uses words the brand already uses. The verb is simple, sometimes embarrassingly so. The phrase reads like something the founder might have said in a bar. When read aloud, the response is *"oh"* not *"interesting"*.

**Test it.** Show the candidate to someone who knows the brand. If they say *"that's reasonable"*, it has failed. If they say *"that's it"* or *"oh"*, it has passed.

---

## 2. Claimed word

**The question.** What single word is this brand claiming as its own?

Every great essence claims a word. Nike claims *do*. Apple claims *different*. BMW claims *driving*. Avis claims *try* (in "We try harder"). The claimed word doesn't have to be unique to the brand — but the brand has to occupy it so thoroughly that the word and the brand fuse in the customer's head.

**How to find it.** Strip the candidate to its strongest single word. If two words feel co-equal, the candidate is fighting itself. If no word feels owned, the candidate is empty.

**The test.** Could the brand build a 20-year campaign around this word? Nike has spent 40 years on *do*. BMW has spent 50 on *driving*. If the claimed word can't carry that weight, the essence is short-term.

**Disqualifiers.** Words too generic to claim (*"better"*, *"smart"*, *"powerful"*). Words owned by another brand (*"think"* belongs to Apple; *"belong"* is being earned by Airbnb). Words that don't index any worldview (*"things"*, *"stuff"*, *"do"* — wait, Nike owns "do," see above; the test is whether the word can carry weight, not whether it's intrinsically powerful).

---

## 3. Music

**The question.** Does it have rhythm, cadence, sonic quality?

Read the candidate aloud. Three times. Does it land on the tongue, or does it stumble? Great essences scan. They have alliteration, rhythm, or stress patterns that make them stick.

**Patterns that work.**

- **Trochees** (strong-weak): *Just Do It. Think Different. Belong Anywhere.* All open with a stressed syllable.
- **Alliteration**: *Fix, Bill, Banked. Built to Build. Founder First.*
- **Two-beat phrases**: *Built. To. Own.* (three syllables, three beats). *Think. Different.* (two strong stresses).
- **Asymmetric pairs**: *Live Más* (2 syllables / 1 syllable). *Just Do It* (1 / 1 / 1, all stressed).

**Patterns that fail.**

- Phrases with too many weak syllables in a row: *"The Intelligence That Belongs"* — five weak syllables before the payoff.
- Phrases that end on a weak syllable: *"Of the Same Drive"* — the last syllable trails, the line doesn't land.
- Phrases where the stress pattern fights the meaning: *"Made to Be Known"* — the stress lands on "be," not "known," which buries the payoff.

**Test it.** Read it aloud. Then read it aloud as a salesperson would, in a meeting. Then read it as a customer would, in a testimonial. Does it survive all three readings?

---

## 4. Layer

**The question.** Is there a second meaning, double-take, or earned ambiguity?

Great essences often have two readings. One obvious, one earned. *Just Do It* is both an instruction to the athlete and a dismissal of excuses. *Think Different* is grammatically wrong (*differently*), and that wrongness is the point — it does what it says.

**Why this matters.** Single-layer essences are flat. Double-layer essences reward re-reading. They earn shelf space in the customer's memory because each encounter unlocks more.

**The test.** Read the candidate. Write down what it means in one sentence. Then ask: is there a second sentence? If the candidate has only one meaning, it's flat. If the second meaning is earned (not forced), it's layered.

**Disqualifiers.** Puns that don't earn their reading. Cleverness that makes the customer work too hard. Ambiguity that obscures meaning rather than enriching it.

**Caution.** Not every great essence is layered. *Belong Anywhere* is single-layer. The skill is to choose: a clean single-layer essence beats a forced double-layer one. Layer is a bonus, not a requirement — but if it's there, it makes the essence harder to forget.

---

## 5. Surprise

**The question.** Would I bet against a competent colleague guessing this independently?

If a competent brand strategist, given the same brief, would have written the same line — the line lacks surprise. Surprise is the property of a phrase that didn't have to exist, but now that it does, feels obvious.

**The test.** Imagine three other strategists writing essences for this brand. How likely is your candidate to appear in their lists? If "likely" — it lacks surprise. If "unlikely but they'd recognise it as right when they saw it" — it has surprise.

**Where surprise comes from.**

- **A verb the category never uses.** The AI category uses *help*, *assist*, *automate*, *scale*. It does not use *own*, *back*, *vouch*, *anchor*. A verb from outside the category brings surprise.
- **A noun the category never claims.** The B2B SaaS category claims *platform*, *solution*, *suite*. It does not claim *partner*, *cofounder*, *anchor*. Reaching outside the category vocabulary is where surprise lives.
- **A frame the category avoids.** Every category has things it won't say (Liquid Death said the word "death" on a water bottle and the category recoiled — that was the surprise). What does this category refuse to say? Can the brand say it?

**Disqualifiers.** Surprise that doesn't serve the brand. Strangeness for the sake of strangeness. Phrases that surprise but feel wrong.

---

## 6. Generative power

**The question.** Does it spawn downstream work?

A brand essence has to feed campaigns, product names, headlines, manifestos, internal rallying cries, sales talk tracks. If it sits inert — pretty on the wall but unable to power anything — it has failed at its job.

**The test.** In Phase 4, sketch three derivatives: a headline, a sub-brand product name, a manifesto opening line. If you can do all three within 5 minutes and each feels natural, the essence is generative. If any of the three feels forced, the essence is decorative.

**Worked example: *Just Do It*.**
- Headline: *"There is no finish line."*
- Sub-brand: *Nike Run Club.* (Run is a *do*.)
- Manifesto opening: *"Tomorrow you're going to ask yourself why you didn't start today."*
The essence powers each effortlessly.

**Worked example: *Belong Anywhere*.**
- Headline: *"Don't go there. Live there."*
- Sub-brand: *Airbnb Experiences.* (Belonging extends beyond stays.)
- Manifesto opening: *"The world is a million places. Each one is somebody's home."*
Again, effortless.

**Counter-example.** *"Excellence Through Innovation"* — try sketching a headline, a sub-brand, a manifesto opening. Each will feel forced. The essence is decorative, not generative.

---

## 7. Mouth test

**The question.** Would a real person — a sales rep, a customer, the founder — say this naturally?

Essences that pass everything else can still fail this test. They look right on paper but feel wrong out loud. Often the failure is one of register — the phrase is too formal, too clever, too marketing-y to live in a real human mouth.

**The test.** Imagine the following three uses and decide if each works:

1. **Sales rep on a Zoom call**: *"Look, we believe in [essence]."* Does the rep stumble? Does the prospect lean in or recoil?
2. **The founder in a press interview**: *"At [company], [essence] is what we're really about."* Does the founder sound like himself, or like a marketing department wrote his lines?
3. **A customer in a testimonial**: *"What I love about [company] is, they really mean [essence]."* Does the customer sound real, or scripted?

If the candidate fails any of these three, the candidate has failed the mouth test.

**Disqualifiers.**

- Words a normal human would not use in conversation: *"empowerment"*, *"actualisation"*, *"verticality"*.
- Phrases that feel like committee output: *"Excellence and Integrity, Together"*.
- Phrases that require setup to land: if the customer has to be primed before the essence makes sense, the essence has failed.

---

## How to use the seven tests in Phase 3

**The cull.** Run all candidates through tests 1, 2, and one of (3, 4, 5). Anything that fails inevitability or claimed word is dead. Anything that has neither music nor layer nor surprise is dead. You should arrive at 5–8 survivors from a starting pool of 60–80.

**The first-pass speed.** Phase 3 should take 15–20 minutes for 60–80 candidates. Speed matters because over-deliberation produces hedging. Trust the gates.

**The promotion.** Of the 5–8 survivors, pick the three with the most distinct worldviews (or the three that survive most of the seven tests) and promote them to Phase 4.

**What to write down for the cuttings room.** For 5–10 of the killed candidates, write a one-line note about why they died. This becomes the cuttings room in the final deliverable. The user gets to see what was considered — and the brevity of the kills is what makes the conviction in Phase 6 land.
