# The economics agents change + the honest tradeoffs

The two halves of the posture. Direction: what improves. Magnitude: what it costs.

## The five economics agents change

When sizing an opportunity, name *which* of these it moves, and how much. Vague "it
saves time" is not sizing.

1. **Cost.** Cost-per-task collapses for suitable work. The unit economics reset — not
   "cheap", but reset. Watch the token line (see tradeoffs).
2. **Speed.** Hours-or-days work becomes minutes. Faster cycle time, not just lower cost.
3. **Scale / volume.** Output that was capacity-bound becomes near-unbounded — more
   variants, always-on coverage, more surface area. (Opinion 2: volume is an
   opportunity, not only a bottleneck.)
4. **Quality-consistency.** A well-governed agent holds a consistent bar across volume
   where humans tire and drift. (Cuts both ways — see brittleness below.)
5. **Learning speed — the big one.** Cheap, fast experiments change the *cost of
   learning*. When a test is near-free to run, you can run speculatively, ahead of
   economic proof. "Designing an agent team is faster than hiring a human one — weeks,
   not months" resets the maths on what's worth trying. This is the economics that
   most changes strategy, and the one the restraint-first skills systematically
   under-weight.

"AI scales whatever sits on top of it" — these gains all multiply the underlying
system, good or bad. Size the opportunity against the system it sits on.

## The honest tradeoffs — "compressed timelines cut both ways"

Never omitted. This is what makes the bullishness trustworthy.

- **Token spend and coordination tax.** A badly-architected team quietly burns more
  than a junior salary's worth of tokens before anyone notices. Loose architecture is
  expensive. Multi-agent coordination has its own overhead, as human teams do.
- **Drift and quality inconsistency.** Without a human consciously holding the quality
  bar, agent teams drift toward plausible-but-wrong — on-brief, off-point. Default
  state, not edge case.
- **Edge-case brittleness.** Agents fail in ways humans don't — confidently, at the
  edges, on the cases the brief didn't anticipate.
- **Maintenance burden.** Agentic systems are not set-and-forget. Models change, tools
  evolve, business needs shift, behaviour drifts. Someone owns ongoing tuning and
  review, or the value decays. Always name the maintenance owner.
- **Snap-decision risk.** Compressed timelines mean less time to see how a system
  behaves in the wild — more snap calls, in a craft that already fights getting-shit-
  done-fast against letting strategy breathe. Speed is a gain and a hazard.

## Where the freed capacity goes

Cheap competence creates abundance; abundance creates sameness; sameness creates demand
for difference — human framing, judgement, review, responsibility (Dan Shipper, *After
Automation*). So hybrid teams don't *reduce* work; they *move* it. The hours freed from
production move up the value chain to framing, taste, oversight and system design. Name
that redeployment explicitly — never present the opportunity as an implied headcount
cut. The work that's left matters more, and demands a muscle most teams haven't built.
