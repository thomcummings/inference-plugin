# Phase 3: Strategic Narrative

How to build the high-altitude company story — a narrative about a shift in the world that creates demand for the company's existence. Based primarily on Andy Raskin's framework, integrated with the StoryBrand "customer as hero" principle and Woden's verbal identity concept.

## 1. What a Strategic Narrative Is (and Is Not)

A strategic narrative is **a story about a change in the world that creates demand for the company.** It is not about the product. It is not a sales pitch. It is not a marketing message.

### It IS:
- A story in people's heads that guides their actions and decisions
- Centred on a shift happening in the buyer's world (not in the company)
- The animating idea that aligns sales, marketing, product, recruiting, and fundraising
- The reason urgency exists — why action is needed now, not later
- The context that makes the company's existence feel inevitable

### It is NOT:
- A product pitch or feature walkthrough
- A positioning statement (positioning is an input to narrative, not the same thing)
- A founding story ("we started in a garage...")
- A mission statement
- "Old way / new way" product comparison (this is a weaker form — see below)

### The Critical Distinction: Old Game / New Game vs. Old Way / New Way

Raskin distinguishes between these two framings:

**"Old Way / New Way"** encourages you to talk primarily about product. The typical version: messy "old way" diagram → clean "new way" diagram. You're saying "our product makes your life better." This is fine but limited. It's a product pitch.

**"Old Game / New Game"** is about a mindset shift happening in the buyer's world that creates demand for the product. It's not about your product at all — it's about a change in the world that the buyer needs to respond to. The product appears as the vehicle for thriving in the new game, not as the point of the story.

Example — Zuora: Not "our billing software is better than spreadsheets." Instead: "We used to live in a world where people wanted to own things. That world is ending. The subscription economy is here. Companies that adapt will thrive. Companies that don't will become the next Blockbuster."

Example (fictional, Ledgerly): "Suppliers used to accept 60-day terms because they had no choice. That's ending: the best suppliers now choose their customers, and they choose the ones who pay on time. Finance teams that still run payables on email and spreadsheets will lose the suppliers they depend on. The ones who make on-time payment automatic will get the best terms and the best partners."

## 2. Why the CEO Must Be Involved

This is non-negotiable in Raskin's methodology, and every experienced practitioner agrees:

- The strategic narrative is a **strategy artefact**, not a marketing artefact
- It must be believed and told by the CEO — not delegated to marketing
- It guides product decisions ("does this product help our customers reach the Promised Land?")
- It guides hiring ("are candidates motivated by this narrative?")
- It guides fundraising ("does this narrative make our market opportunity feel inevitable?")

If the CEO doesn't lead the narrative development, it becomes "a marketing exercise that goes nowhere" (as one CMO put it to Raskin). The CEO should be the person in charge of building the narrative, supported by a small leadership team.

### In a Consulting Context

When working as a fractional CMO or external advisor:
- Frame this as a strategy project, not a marketing project
- Insist on CEO participation in at least the key working sessions
- Present the narrative for CEO sign-off, not just marketing sign-off
- Position yourself as facilitating the CEO's story, not writing it for them

## 3. The Five Elements

These are the building blocks of a great strategic narrative, derived from Raskin's analysis of what makes the best company stories work. They follow the same narrative structure as epic films and myths — this is intentional.

### Element 1: Name the Shift

**What it is:** A big, relevant change happening in the world. Not in your product — in your buyer's world. This is the "new game" that creates winners and losers.

**Why it works:** Starting with a shift in the world (rather than a problem to solve) creates shared context. Prospects open up because you're discussing their reality, not pitching your solution.

**Quality criteria:**
- Is this shift real and verifiable? (not manufactured)
- Does the buyer recognise it as happening in their world?
- Does it create both opportunity and risk?
- Is it big enough to matter but specific enough to be credible?
- Is it happening now (creating urgency)?

**Framework for discovering the shift:**
- What changes in your customers' world are creating opportunity and risk?
- What assumptions that used to be safe are now dangerous?
- What practices that used to work are now failing?
- If you ask customers "what's changing in your world?" — what do they say?

**Critical: harvest the language of the shift from actual customers and prospects.** Don't invent it at a whiteboard. The most powerful shifts use the exact words customers use to describe what they're experiencing.

### Element 2: Show the Stakes

**What it is:** Make clear that this shift creates winners and losers. Adapting leads to success. Ignoring the shift leads to ruin. The stakes are real.

**Why it works:** Stakes create urgency. Without stakes, the shift is interesting but not actionable. With stakes, inaction becomes risky.

**Quality criteria:**
- Are the stakes concrete and believable?
- Can you point to real examples of winners and losers?
- Are the consequences of inaction specific, not vague?

**Avoid:** Don't label the prospect's current approach as "flawed" or "wrong" — this puts them on the defensive. Instead, show that what used to work (the "old game") is being disrupted by forces beyond their control. Empathy, not accusation.

### Element 3: Tease the Promised Land

**What it is:** The happy future for those who adapt to the shift. Not your product — the destination. What life looks like for companies/people who thrive in the new game.

**Why it works:** The Promised Land is your commitment to prospects. It's the ROI of the relationship, framed as a future state they want to reach.

**Quality criteria:**
- Is the Promised Land desirable? (Would the prospect want this future?)
- Is it specific enough to be credible?
- Is it achievable? (Not so grand that it feels unrealistic)
- Can you describe it in the prospect's language, not yours?

**Format tip:** Raskin notes that framing the Promised Land as a question can be powerful. "What if you could ___?" opens a conversation. A declarative statement closes it.

### Element 4: Introduce the Magic Gifts

**What it is:** The capabilities that help people reach the Promised Land. This is where your company/product enters the story — not as the hero, but as the guide (StoryBrand principle) who provides the tools for the journey.

**Why it works:** After establishing the shift, stakes, and destination, the audience is primed to hear about solutions. Capabilities positioned in the context of the narrative feel necessary rather than optional.

**Quality criteria:**
- Do the capabilities clearly connect to reaching the Promised Land?
- Are they presented as enablers, not as the point of the story?
- Do they map to your differentiated capabilities from positioning?
- Are there 2-4 capabilities? (Not a feature list — capability clusters)

**Connection to positioning:** The "magic gifts" should map directly to the payoffs from the positioning canvas. If positioning identified three payoffs, those become the three magic gifts in the narrative.

### Element 5: Present the Evidence

**What it is:** Proof that you can make the story real. Customer success stories, data, analyst validation, market evidence.

**Why it works:** The narrative makes a promise. Prospects are rightly sceptical. Evidence is how you earn credibility.

**Quality criteria:**
- Is the evidence from someone similar to the prospect?
- Is it specific and measurable?
- Does it show the journey (before/after), not just the destination?
- Is it recent and relevant?

**Best evidence types:**
1. Customer success stories (most powerful — similar company, similar situation, reached the Promised Land)
2. Market data supporting the shift
3. Analyst or expert validation
4. Company metrics (if they demonstrate the narrative in action)

## 4. Building the Narrative: Step by Step

### Step 1: Gather Inputs

Before building narrative, ensure you have:
- Positioning output (competitive alternatives, differentiated value, best-fit customers)
- Brand foundations (purpose, vision, mission — from Phase 2 of this skill)
- Customer language (what they say about changes in their world)
- Competitive landscape (what stories competitors tell)

### Step 2: Discover the Shift

Workshop approach (recommended for client engagements):

1. Ask the leadership team: "What's changing in your customers' world that creates both opportunity and risk?"
2. Collect 5-10 candidate shifts
3. For each, test: Is it real? Recognised by buyers? Creating urgency? Big enough? Specific enough?
4. Narrow to 1-2 candidates
5. Validate language with actual customer conversations if possible

Solo approach (when working from research/documents):

1. Review customer interviews, win/loss data, and market research for shifts customers describe
2. Review the company's positioning topics and brand positioning POVs for implicit shift narratives
3. Look for the pattern: what used to work → what's changing → what now works
4. Draft 2-3 candidate shift statements
5. Test each against the quality criteria above

### Step 3: Develop Stakes, Promised Land, Magic Gifts, Evidence

For the chosen shift, work through elements 2-5 sequentially. Each builds on the previous.

Produce a first draft in this format:

```
THE SHIFT
[1-2 sentences describing the change in the world]

THE STAKES
[1-2 sentences on what's at risk / winners vs. losers]
[Optional: 1-2 concrete examples]

THE PROMISED LAND
[1-2 sentences describing the future for those who adapt]

THE MAGIC GIFTS
[Gift 1]: [Brief description of the capability cluster]
[Gift 2]: [Brief description]
[Gift 3]: [Brief description]

THE EVIDENCE
[1-2 strongest proof points]
```

### Step 4: Refine and Test

Read the narrative aloud. Does it flow? Does it build tension and then resolve it? Does the company enter the story naturally, or does it feel forced?

Key refinement questions:
- Would a prospect hear this and say "tell me more" or "I know, get to the product"?
- Does the shift feel like news, or does it feel like a lecture?
- Is the Promised Land something they want, or something you want for them?
- Do the magic gifts feel like natural consequences of the narrative, or bolt-on features?

## 5. Verbal Identity

Adapted from Woden's messaging hierarchy framework. Verbal identity defines the **1-5 specific terms and phrases the brand should own in customers' minds.**

### Why It Matters

Research shows consumers instinctively associate specific terms with particular brands, and the connection is singular — any given term has only one brand association for a buyer. Owning terms is critical at the top of the funnel: buyers are more likely to remember these terms than the brand name itself.

### Developing Verbal Identity

The terms should be derived from the strategic narrative, specifically from:
- The Shift (what's the term for the change? e.g., "subscription economy" for Zuora)
- The Promised Land (what's the term for the destination?)
- The Magic Gifts (are any capabilities distinctive enough to own a term?)

**Quality criteria:**
- Is each term unique to your brand? (Not generic industry jargon)
- Is each term memorable and repeatable?
- Can each term be used consistently across all storytelling?
- Do the terms together tell a coherent story?

### Examples

Woden's own verbal identity: "StoryKernel," "strategic narrative," "essential brands"
Zuora: "subscription economy"
Ledgerly (fictional): "paid on time," "no chasing," "the supplier-first finance team"

### Usage Rule

The verbal identity terms should be used consistently and repeatedly across all narrative applications — sales decks, website, content, social media, executive talks. Consistency creates association. Inconsistency wastes the investment.

## 6. Testing the Narrative

### Internal Test

- Can 5 different people in the company tell the narrative consistently? (Not word-for-word — but the same story beats)
- Does product development reference the narrative when making roadmap decisions?
- Do recruiters use the narrative in pitch conversations?

### External Test

- Do prospects open up more when you lead with the shift vs. leading with the product?
- Do sales cycles shorten when the narrative is used in first meetings?
- Do prospects say things like "I need this" rather than "that's interesting"?

### Laja's Messaging Validation (Applied to Narrative)

Test the narrative against Peep Laja's four criteria:
1. **Clarity:** Does the audience understand the shift and its implications?
2. **Relevance:** Do they recognise it as happening in their world?
3. **Value:** Do they see the Promised Land as desirable and achievable?
4. **Differentiation:** Is this a narrative only your company can tell credibly?

## 7. Translating Narrative into Artefacts

The strategic narrative should power multiple downstream artefacts:

**Sales Deck:** The most direct translation. Raskin works primarily via sales decks because they're the artefact that actually gets used. The five elements map directly to a deck structure.

**Website Hero Section:** The shift becomes the framing for the homepage. Not "We do X" but "The world is changing — here's how to thrive."

**Investor Pitch:** Same narrative, different emphasis on market size and evidence.

**Recruiting Pitch:** Same narrative, emphasis on the Promised Land as the company's mission and what it means to work here.

**Content Strategy:** The shift and stakes generate thought leadership topics. The Promised Land generates aspirational content. The magic gifts generate product-related content.

**Messaging Framework:** The narrative becomes the "roof" of the message house. Value pillars connect to the magic gifts. This is the primary handoff to the Messaging Framework skill.

## 8. Common Pitfalls

**Making the company the hero:** The strongest instinct to fight. The company is the guide, not the hero. The customer/buyer is the hero navigating the shift. The company provides the tools. (StoryBrand principle)

**Starting with the product:** The most common mistake. The narrative should run for 30-60 seconds before the product is even mentioned. If you can't describe the shift without referencing your product, the shift isn't independent enough.

**Copying another company's narrative:** Raskin warns about this explicitly. A company took Zuora's deck, swapped in their product, and prospects asked them to skip ahead. The shift must be authentic to your company's market and customers, not borrowed.

**Too much "old game" detail:** Dwelling on what's wrong with the status quo puts prospects on the defensive. Show the shift, acknowledge the stakes, then move to the Promised Land. Spend more time on the future than the past.

**Narrative without alignment:** The narrative only works when the entire company tells the same story. Sales using a different narrative than marketing, or the CEO telling a different story than the sales deck, destroys the effect. Company-wide alignment is the point, not a nice-to-have.

**Conflating narrative with messaging:** The strategic narrative is the high-altitude story. Messaging is how that story gets expressed at different levels (company, product, feature) for different audiences. The narrative informs messaging, but they're different artefacts at different levels of specificity.

**Thinking narrative is "done":** A strategic narrative should be revisited when the market shifts significantly, when the company enters new markets, or when the narrative stops creating urgency. It's more stable than messaging but less stable than purpose. Typical lifecycle: 2-5 years before significant revision.
