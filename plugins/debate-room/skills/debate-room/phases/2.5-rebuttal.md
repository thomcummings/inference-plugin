# Phase 2.5: Rebuttal round (default for decision questions)

This phase is the default for any question where the user is trying to make a decision, and in practice it's where the room actually converges on an answer. Phase 2 produces sharp independent takes. Phase 2.5 is where personas hear each other, concede or hold, and the *room* (not just the orchestrator) surfaces the real answer.

**Default behaviour: run it.** Unless you have a clear reason to skip, run the rebuttal round. Three runs of stress-testing the skill showed that phase 2.5 is consistently where the actual decision forms — skipping it produces a weaker synthesis that reads as "here are N smart takes, pick one" rather than a single recommendation backed by the room's collective movement.

**Skip it only if ALL of the following are true** (not "any of" — *all* of):

1. The room has converged on **every** dimension of the question — direction, outcome shape, which wedge, timing, load-bearing assumptions, and recommended action. Not just direction. Not just "most" dimensions. *Every* dimension.
2. The stance persona (contrarian-skeptic or equivalent) has no un-rebutted attack still on the table.
3. There are no specific numerical or empirical claims in phase 2 that the room couldn't verify and that might bias the synthesis if unchallenged.
4. The question is either a quick gut-check, or the user explicitly asked for the first-round synthesis only.

If *any* of these four conditions fails, run the rebuttal. "Partial convergence" is not a skip condition — it is the single most common situation where rebuttal adds value, because it's exactly where the room has enough shape to pressure-test but not enough agreement to commit. If you find yourself writing a sentence of the form *"the room converged on X but diverged on Y — that disagreement is the value of this run,"* **stop: that disagreement is the raw material for rebuttal, not a substitute for it.** Running rebuttal on a divergent room is the entire point of the phase.

**Always run it if:** the question has real tradeoffs, the user is trying to break a deadlock, phase 2 produced substantive disagreement, the user gave new information mid-debate, or you (the orchestrator) aren't sure how to weigh the competing positions.

## What the rebuttal round does that phase 2 can't

Phase 2's value is *independence*. Personas can't anchor on each other because they haven't seen each other. That's the right first move.

But independence also has a cost: no persona gets the chance to respond to the specific argument that would have changed their mind. The copywriter might have caught a cadence problem the strategist couldn't hear; in phase 2, the strategist never hears it. In phase 2.5, they do — and they either concede, hold their ground with a sharper reason, or sharpen their position to address the attack.

**The output you're looking for is movement.** Who conceded? Who held? Who sharpened? That movement is data — it tells the synthesis phase where the room's centre of gravity *actually* is, not where the orchestrator thinks it is.

## Check for convergence drift before dispatching

Before writing the rebuttal briefs, look at phase 2 and ask one question: **does the user's question contain specific identifiable options (e.g. "should we pick A, B, or C"), and is the room converging against one of them?**

If both are true — the question has specific options, and three or more personas are dismissing the same option — assign one persona to **steelman the losing option** in the rebuttal round. Pick the persona most plausibly sympathetic to that position (for a strategy-execution question where the room is dismissing "do nothing dramatic," the founder-operator or sales-leader is the natural fit; for a creative question where the room is dismissing the safe choice, the performance-marketer is). That persona's rebuttal brief becomes a focused defence of the losing option, with an explicit instruction: *"Give the strongest honest case for [option X]. Not a strawman. Not a polite acknowledgement before dismissing it. The actual best argument for doing this."*

If either condition fails — the question is open-ended with no specific options, or the room is genuinely split — **skip the steelman** and run the standard rebuttal. Do not invent options to argue against. A forced steelman on an open-ended question produces artificial friction that has nothing to do with the actual question.

Why this matters: when a single model is running all N personas, the model's own priors can leak into all of them, producing a polite consensus where five personas quietly dismiss the same option without any of them genuinely engaging with its merits. The steelman exists to structurally prevent that — it forces the room to hear the best case for the losing option out loud, and the concede/hold/sharpen moves in the rest of the rebuttal round have to contend with it. It's the cheapest structural cure for simulated consensus on a discrete-options question.

The steelmanning persona still writes their regular rebuttal response (concede / hold / sharpen) on their *own* round 1 position — the steelman is an additional instruction layered on top, not a replacement for their normal move.

## Forced minority hold (the general non-convergence rule)

The steelman above is for questions with **discrete options**. This rule is for the more common failure mode: the room converges on a general direction without ever having had discrete options to pick between. Both rules target simulated consensus, but they apply in different cases — do not confuse them.

Before writing the rebuttal briefs, ask: **after phase 2, is the room broadly converging with fewer than one major unresolved disagreement remaining?** Count only *substantive* disagreements — clashes about what the right answer actually is. Disagreements about emphasis, timing, or which persona gets the sharpest phrasing do not count.

If the room is broadly converged and the question is not a gut-check, **assume convergence drift**. Assign one persona (ideally *not* the contrarian-skeptic — they are already doing this job, and doubling up produces noise) to hold a minority position with sharpened reasoning. Pick the persona whose axis or worldview would most plausibly disagree with the emerging consensus, and write their rebuttal brief with this additional instruction:

> *"The room is converging on [X]. I don't believe that convergence is real — I think it's the single-writer model harmonising. Your job in this round is to hold a minority position against [X], sharpened with your actual worldview. Not contrarianism for its own sake — the strongest honest version of 'the room is wrong.' If after trying honestly you cannot produce a sharpened argument that isn't forced, say so explicitly: 'I tried to hold and could not, because [specific reason]. The convergence on this point is real.' That is also a valid output."*

This last escape hatch matters. The point of the rule is to test convergence, not to manufacture contrarianism. Sometimes the room genuinely has converged because the answer is clear — and when that happens, the forced minority hold should fail honestly and say so. A failed forced hold is *still useful data*: it's the room testing its own convergence and confirming it under pressure, which is stronger evidence than convergence that was never tested at all.

**Steelman vs forced minority hold — when to use which:**

| Situation | Use |
|---|---|
| Question has discrete options (A, B, C) and room is converging against one | **Steelman** — defend the losing option specifically |
| Question is open-ended and room is broadly converged on a direction | **Forced minority hold** — hold a minority position against the emerging consensus |
| Room is genuinely split after phase 2 | **Neither** — run standard rebuttal, the friction is already real |
| Question is a gut-check | **Neither** — skip rebuttal entirely |

Use one or the other or neither, depending on the case. Do not use both in the same run — they attack the same failure mode from different angles and stacking them produces noise.

## How to run it

Dispatch each persona again as a separate subagent, in parallel, same as phase 2. But the brief is different.

### Rebuttal brief template

```
You are [persona], returning for round 2 of a structured debate.

=== YOUR PERSONA ===
<paste the persona file>

=== YOUR ROUND 1 POSITION ===
<paste that persona's own round 1 response verbatim>

=== WHAT THE OTHER EXPERTS SAID ===
<paste all other personas' round 1 responses verbatim, in full>

=== WHERE YOUR POSITION WAS VULNERABLE ===
<this is the most important part — the orchestrator names the specific
attacks and gaps from the other personas that your round-1 position did
not address. Be direct. "The copywriter flagged that [X]; you did not
address this. The contrarian pointed out that [Y]; your assumption about
[Z] is the weak point. The end-customer's reaction to [W] suggests your
framing might be wrong." Do not soften. If the persona has no vulnerabilities
you can see, say so — but look hard first.>

=== NEW INFORMATION FROM THE USER (if any) ===
<if the user provided clarifications, context updates, or new constraints
between round 1 and round 2, paste them here. This is the official hook
for mid-debate user input.>

=== YOUR JOB IN THIS ROUND ===
You must pick exactly one of three moves. **HOLD and SHARPEN are the
defaults. CONCEDE is the exception and requires justification.**

1. **HOLD** — maintain your position with a SHARPER reason than you gave
   in round 1 — specifically, a reason that addresses the strongest
   attack you just read. "I still think X" is not enough. "I still
   think X, and here is why [attacking persona]'s objection is wrong /
   doesn't apply / is outweighed by Y" is the bar. This is a legitimate
   and common move, especially when your worldview is genuinely different
   from the attacker's.

2. **SHARPEN** — your core position is right but some aspect of it was
   wrong or under-specified. Revise. Name what you got wrong in round 1
   and what the new version is. This is the most common valid move.

3. **CONCEDE** (exception only) — acknowledge that an argument from
   another persona, or new information from the user, has genuinely
   changed your mind. Conceding is a sign of intellectual honesty, but
   it is also the path of least resistance in a written rebuttal, so
   you are required to justify it: state in one sentence *why* the
   counter-argument was strong enough to move you off a position you
   held sharply in round 1. "Good point, agreed" is not a concession,
   it is a capitulation, and capitulations are banned in this round.
   If you cannot write a sentence of the form "I'm conceding because
   [specific argument] showed that [specific assumption I made] was
   wrong," you are not actually conceding — you are holding weakly.
   Pick HOLD or SHARPEN instead.

Do NOT simply restate your round 1 position. "On reflection I still think..."
without engaging the specific attacks is a failure. If you find yourself
writing that, stop and re-read what the other personas actually said.

=== OUTPUT FORMAT ===

**Move**
(CONCEDE / HOLD / SHARPEN — pick one)

**What moved me (or didn't)**
(Which specific argument from which specific persona did you engage with?
Name names. If you held, what made the attack fail? If you conceded,
what made it land? If you sharpened, what did you get wrong?)

**Updated position**
(Your round 2 position in 2–4 sentences. Even if you held, state it
fresh — the room has moved, the context is different.)

**What the room as a whole is getting wrong**
(With the benefit of having seen everyone's round 1, what do you now
think the whole room is missing? This is a new field for round 2 —
use it.)
```

## When subagents return

Collect everything verbatim. Do not sanitise.

Then compile a **movement map** — a short structured record of what happened:

- Who conceded, to whom, on what
- Who held, and whether their sharper reason actually addressed the attack
- Who sharpened, and what the delta from round 1 is
- Any new "what the whole room is missing" insights

This movement map is an artefact. It goes into the transcript alongside the round 1 and round 2 responses. The synthesis phase uses it to determine where the room's centre of gravity actually landed.

## Guardrails

- **Don't run more than one rebuttal round** per question. Diminishing returns kick in hard, and by round 3 personas are just performing their own character rather than engaging with the substance.
- **Preserve round 1 in the transcript.** The user should be able to see what each persona thought *before* they heard the others. That's part of the value.
- **If the rebuttal round produces no movement** — everyone just holds with slightly sharper reasons — that's still useful data: it means the disagreements are fundamental, not resolvable by more information. Say so in the synthesis.
- **The last-word rule.** If persona A attacks persona B's position and B does not respond to the attack in their rebuttal, that is a concession by silence and must be logged in the movement map as such. The orchestrator is not allowed to quietly let an unanswered attack drop. Either the attacked persona must address it, or the movement map records "B did not respond to A's [specific attack]; treating as silent concession." This prevents the polite dynamic where uncomfortable attacks vanish between rounds because no one picked them back up.
- **The concession-rate smell test.** If more than half the room concedes in the rebuttal round, something has gone wrong — either phase 2 was weak and the personas didn't commit hard enough, or the "good point, agreed" dynamic is leaking through despite the exception rule. A rebuttal round where everyone concedes is a rebuttal round that didn't happen. If you see this, re-dispatch the conceding personas with the justification requirement reinforced: *"Your concession in round 2 did not include a specific sentence explaining why your round 1 assumption was wrong. Either write that sentence or switch to HOLD / SHARPEN."*

## When to skip the rebuttal round entirely

Phase 2.5 is default-on, but there are cases where skipping is correct: a quick gut-check question where the user just wants a fast multi-perspective take, a question where phase 2 produced genuine clean convergence with nothing left to resolve, or an explicit user request to skip rebuttal. In those cases, note in the transcript that you chose to skip and why, then proceed to phase 3.

Do not skip rebuttal just because phase 2 "feels done." The whole point of two runs of stress-testing is that the rebuttal round consistently produces more decision-useful output than the orchestrator expects. Default to running it.

Then proceed to phase 3 (tension mapping, now with both rounds of data).
